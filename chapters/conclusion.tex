\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{ch:conclusions}

This thesis set out to understand whether 3D geometric structures and natural language representations share enough common ground to be aligned without heavy supervision. The motivation was straightforward: paired 3D-text datasets are expensive to build, so a method that could sidestep this requirement would be genuinely useful. What emerged instead was not a success story in the usual sense, but something arguably more informative --- a detailed picture of exactly where and why zero-shot geometric alignment breaks down, and what that breakdown tells us about the latent spaces neural networks actually learn.

\section{Summary of Results}
\label{sec:summary}

The results split cleanly into two camps. On one side, supervised methods work well. Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) paired with an affine transformation --- the linear baseline replicated from the original paper --- reached $35.7\%$ Top-5 retrieval accuracy on the PointNet and \gls{clip} pair. Riemannian Metric Learning (RML), introduced here as an original non-linear extension, came close behind at $27.2\%$. Neither method is magic: both depend entirely on having $30{,}000$ labelled anchor pairs available to learn the mapping.

On the other side, everything unsupervised collapsed. We tried five distinct \gls{ot} variants --- \gls{sgw}, \gls{risgw}, \gls{ssrisgw}, \gls{fgw}, and \gls{lrgw} --- and none of the purely unsupervised ones broke $3.2\%$ Top-5 (Sliced GW was the best of a bad lot). Even Fused GW, which sneaks in a small amount of anchor supervision, only reached $4.4\%$ --- roughly $8\times$ below what CCA+Affine achieves with full supervision. This wasn't a case of one bad run or a lucky/unlucky seed: the thirteen-test ablation study methodically checked implementation correctness, hyperparameter sensitivity, and library-level consistency, and none of it moved the needle. The conclusion that emerges is fairly stark. PointNet and CLIP, trained on completely different sensory data with no shared objective, simply do not converge to structurally isometric spaces. Asking an unsupervised solver to minimise the structural gap between two non-isometric spaces doesn't approximate a good alignment --- it actively destroys the semantic correspondences that do exist, which is precisely why zero-shot geometric alignment proved impossible here.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

A few caveats are worth being upfront about.

The most practical one concerns scale. Exact Gromov-Wasserstein scales at $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$, which ruled out computing dense transport plans across the full dataset. As detailed in Section~\ref{subsec:fused_gw}, this is what forced the unsupervised and semi-supervised GW variants down to $1{,}000$--$5{,}000$ anchors rather than the full $30{,}000$ used for CCA+Affine, and it's also why low-rank approximations and sliced 1D projections were necessary throughout. Could this have hidden a better solution somewhere? We don't think so --- the fine-grained epsilon sweeps and the anchor-count grid search both showed no improvement beyond $n{=}5{,}000$ samples, and the flat cost landscape observed in Tests 3 and 13 is a fairly strong signal that there was nothing better to find. Still, these approximations smooth the transport matrix by construction, and it's conceivable that some fine-grained local structure got averaged away in the process.

A second limitation sits with the data itself. Objaverse doesn't come with human-written captions; Cap3D generates them synthetically. That means every text encoder in this thesis was aligning against descriptive, somewhat formulaic captions rather than the messier, more varied language a person would actually use. Whether that matters for the isometry question is an open question in its own right.

Third, almost every encoder used here was frozen and pre-trained --- with one deliberate exception. SparseConv was run with random, untrained weights (Section~\ref{subsec:3d_encoders}), specifically so we could isolate what architecture alone contributes versus what comes from learned features. CLIP, meanwhile, brings enormous visual grounding to the table but was never exposed to 3D voxels or point clouds during its own training. Whether a 3D backbone with real geometric priors --- something more structurally aware than PointNet's fairly basic global pooling --- would narrow the isometry gap is a question this thesis simply couldn't answer within its scope.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future_work}

If there's one clear takeaway from the negative results here, it's that trying to align frozen, independently-trained spaces after the fact is probably not the right strategy going forward. A few directions seem more promising.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Joint Contrastive Pre-training:} rather than freezing two encoders and hoping their geometries line up, train them together from the start, with a contrastive objective that explicitly penalises non-isometric structure as it emerges. If the co-location is baked in during training, there's no need for a complicated post-hoc transport step at all.
    
    \item \textbf{Relaxed Topological Constraints:} for cases where post-hoc alignment really is the only option, it may help to drop the requirement of global isometry altogether. Partial, localised transport mechanisms, or heavily regularised Fused GW variants that lean on weak priors like coarse category clustering, could plausibly guide the transport plan without needing tens of thousands of exact pairwise labels.
    
    \item \textbf{LLMs as Explicit Spatial Bridges:} instead of mapping raw geometry straight onto continuous text embeddings, an intermediate, discrete representation might help. A Large Language Model could be prompted to describe a 3D object structurally --- a scene graph, say, or a set of bounding-box relationships --- and alignment could target that explicit spatial logic instead of a plain descriptive caption. That kind of structured intermediate seems more likely to carry genuine isomorphism with 3D geometry than free-form text does.
\end{itemize}

None of this changes the basic finding: unsupervised optimal transport, as tested here, cannot bridge 3D and text on its own. But mapping out exactly where that boundary sits --- and ruling out the more mundane explanations for the failure one by one --- has been informative in its own right, and hopefully useful for anyone thinking about where to invest effort next in multimodal 3D-language work.
