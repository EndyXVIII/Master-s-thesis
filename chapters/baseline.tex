\chapter{Baseline: Replication of Hadgi et al., CVPR 2025}
\label{ch:baseline}

Before we can say anything meaningful about unsupervised optimal transport, we need a reliable performance ceiling to measure it against. That's what this chapter sets out to establish: we replicate the supervised linear alignment framework introduced by \textcite{hadgi2025escaping}. Their work showed that raw 3D and text latent spaces, though inherently disjoint, share underlying geometric correlations that a subspace projection can successfully bridge. This chapter walks through our replication of their methodology on the Objaverse dataset and sets the quantitative benchmark that all our subsequent Optimal Transport experiments get measured against.

\section{CCA + Affine Pipeline}
\label{sec:cca_affine}

The basic premise behind this baseline is that mapping high-dimensional features directly across modalities is notoriously hard, mostly because of modality-specific noise and mismatched spatial dimensionalities. To get around this, the alignment splits into two steps: a dimensionality reduction phase, followed by a direct linear translation.

Working on our pre-defined subset of $n_A = 30{,}000$ paired anchors, we first fit a Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) model. \gls{cca} looks for linear projections that map both the 3D features ($X$) and the text features ($Y$) into a shared $k$-dimensional space where their cross-correlation is maximised. Following the original authors' setup, we fix the target subspace dimensionality to $k = 50$. This drastic reduction acts as an information filter, stripping away irrelevant noise and isolating whatever core semantic signal the two spaces actually have in common.

Once the data sits in this shared, lower-dimensional subspace, we compute an affine transformation $T(x) = Rx + b$ via standard least-squares regression. This learns to map the reduced 3D coordinates directly onto the text coordinates. At inference time, any unseen 3D query gets projected through the learned \gls{cca} weights first, then translated via the affine map into text space, where standard nearest-neighbour retrieval can be evaluated.

The numbers make the impact of the text encoder's prior training regime pretty obvious. When PointNet is paired with the OpenCLIP text encoder, the system hits a peak Top-5 retrieval accuracy of $35.7\%$. Try mapping the exact same PointNet geometric features onto \gls{bert} or \gls{roberta} instead, and performance drops off noticeably --- and all three still clear the random-guessing baseline by a wide margin: with $500$ query pairs, chance-level Top-1 accuracy sits at $\approx 1/500 = 0.2\%$, so even the weakest supervised pairing (PointNet$\times$BERT, $8.5\%$ Top-1) is more than $40\times$ above what pure luck would produce.

Figures~\ref{fig:baseline_pointnet} and~\ref{fig:baseline_sparseconv} break
this same table down visually, one figure per 3D backbone, which makes a
few patterns much easier to spot than scanning table rows. Within each
figure, look first at how the four bars for a given text encoder relate
to each other: accuracy climbs steadily from Match (the strictest
criterion, requiring the single correct pairing) through Top-1, Top-5, and
up to Top-10. That ordering isn't a finding so much as a sanity check ---
relaxing the retrieval criterion from ``find the one correct match'' to
``find it somewhere in the top ten'' can only ever make the task easier,
never harder, so this monotonic climb is exactly what a working pipeline
should produce. What's more interesting is the ordering \emph{across} the
three text encoders, which holds in both figures despite covering very
different 3D backbones: CLIP-paired bars are consistently the tallest,
RoBERTa comes next, and BERT trails behind. That consistency, showing up
independently of whether the 3D encoder was trained at all, is a fairly
strong signal that something about CLIP's text space specifically ---
rather than some interaction with PointNet's particular features --- makes
it more compatible with this kind of linear alignment.

The gap between the two figures is worth pausing on too. Notice that the
y-axis in Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_sparseconv} only goes up to about
$24\%$, versus roughly $50\%$ in Figure~\ref{fig:baseline_pointnet} ---
plotting both backbones on the very same axis would have squashed all six
SparseConv bars down into an unreadable sliver at the bottom, so each
figure is scaled independently to its own data range. That difference in
scale is itself the headline result: switching from a pre-trained PointNet
backbone to a SparseConv backbone with entirely random, untrained weights
costs roughly two-thirds of the Top-5 accuracy across the board, dropping
from the $25$--$36\%$ range down to $10$--$13\%$. What survives that drop
is arguably more interesting than the drop itself, though: SparseConv,
despite never having learned anything at all, still manages double-digit
Top-5 accuracy with every text encoder, and the same CLIP $>$ RoBERTa $>$
BERT ordering persists even here. We come back to why that happens in the
discussion below.

\paragraph{Note on run-to-run variance.} Because the \gls{cca} anchor/query
split is re-sampled per run, independent re-executions of the \gls{cca}+Affine
baseline used as a reference point in companion experiments (Chapters
\ref{ch:nonlinear} and \ref{ch:gw_alignment}) yielded slightly different
Top-5 values on PointNet$\times$\gls{clip}: $34.1\%$ in the run paired with the
KCCA/RML evaluation, and $33.2\%$ in the run paired with the cross-library
GW verification (Test 10, Section~\ref{subsec:library_analysis}), against
the $35.7\%$ reported here as the representative value averaged over 3
seeds in Table~\ref{tab:linear_baseline_results}. This variation
($\pm 2$ percentage points) reflects the inherent stochasticity of the \gls{cca}
fitting procedure across different random splits rather than a
methodological inconsistency, and all comparative claims made throughout
this thesis (performance ratios, method rankings) are robust to this range
of variation.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:baseline_discussion}

The success of the \gls{cca} + Affine pipeline tells us a few interesting things about how these neural networks structure their latent spaces. First and most obviously, it backs up the idea that language models trained alongside images --- \gls{clip} being the case in point --- end up with a latent space that's inherently more ``geometric.'' Because \gls{clip} is forced to align text with 2D visual structures during its massive pre-training phase, its text embeddings hold onto a spatial logic that turns out to be much easier for a 3D encoder to map onto via a simple linear translation.

The more surprising finding, though, comes out of the SparseConv experiments. Even with the 3D backbone initialised entirely with random weights and never pre-trained at all, the alignment pipeline still manages $13.1\%$ Top-5 accuracy with \gls{clip}. That happens because a random projection of the 3D points still preserves some baseline degree of spatial covariance --- the geometry isn't meaningful in any learned sense, but it isn't noise either. The \gls{cca} step acts as a powerful feature selector here, mining out whatever raw geometric correlations exist and discarding the rest, which lets the affine map latch onto a weak but statistically real alignment signal even without any actual learning on the 3D side.

These supervised results are genuinely promising, but they lean heavily on having 30,000 perfectly paired anchors available to learn the projection matrices in the first place. In real-world scenarios, curating a dataset of paired 3D-text data at that scale is prohibitively expensive and time-consuming. That operational bottleneck is really what motivates the core question this thesis is built around: can we bypass the need for paired anchors altogether? The next chapter drops the supervised \gls{cca} + Affine pipeline entirely and tries to align the raw representational spaces using purely unsupervised Optimal Transport instead, testing whether these spaces have enough inherent structural isometry to be matched without any explicit supervision at all.
