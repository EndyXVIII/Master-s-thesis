\chapter{Non-Linear Supervised Alignment Methods}
\label{ch:nonlinear}

While the CCA + Affine baseline of Chapter~\ref{ch:baseline} establishes a
strong supervised linear upper bound, it is inherently constrained to learning
affine transformations between the two modalities. This chapter investigates
two non-linear extensions that operate within the same supervised paradigm
--- leveraging the same 30,000 ground-truth anchor pairs --- but exploit
more expressive, non-linear geometric structures to potentially close the
remaining performance gap.

Both methods are evaluated under identical experimental conditions to those
of Chapter~\ref{ch:baseline}: $n_A = 30{,}000$ anchor pairs, $k = 50$ CCA
subspace, 500 held-out query pairs, averaged over three independent random seeds.

\section{Kernel CCA with Nyström Approximation}
\label{sec:kcca}

\subsection{Motivation}
\label{subsec:kcca_motivation}

Standard CCA discovers only linear correlations between two spaces. However,
the relationship between 3D geometric representations and natural language
embeddings may contain non-linear structure that a linear projection cannot
capture. Kernel CCA (KCCA) extends CCA by implicitly mapping features into
a higher-dimensional Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS) via a kernel
function, allowing the discovery of non-linear correlations without explicitly
computing the high-dimensional feature map.

\subsection{Implementation}
\label{subsec:kcca_implementation}

KCCA replaces the dot product in the standard covariance computation with
a kernel evaluation:
\begin{equation}
    K(x, x') = \exp\!\left(-\gamma \|x - x'\|^2\right)
    \label{eq:rbf_kernel}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma = 0.1$ is the RBF bandwidth parameter. The resulting kernel
matrix $K \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ captures non-linear similarity
between samples in the original feature space.

Computing KCCA exactly requires inverting an $n \times n$ matrix, which
scales as $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ --- computationally infeasible at $n = 30{,}000$
anchors. To address this, we employ the \textbf{Nyström approximation}
\cite{williams2001nystrom}, which approximates the full kernel matrix using
a randomly selected subset of $m = 500$ landmark points:
\begin{equation}
    K \approx K_{nm}\, K_{mm}^{-1}\, K_{mn}
    \label{eq:nystrom}
\end{equation}
where $K_{nm} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$ contains kernel evaluations between
all $n$ training points and the $m$ landmarks, and $K_{mm} \in \mathbb{R}^{m
\times m}$ is the kernel matrix restricted to the landmarks. This reduces the
computational cost to $\mathcal{O}(m^2 n)$, making KCCA tractable at full
dataset scale.

\section{Riemannian Metric Learning}
\label{sec:rml}

\subsection{Motivation}
\label{subsec:rml_motivation}

Rather than learning a mapping between the two spaces, Riemannian Metric
Learning (RML) takes a different approach: it learns a \emph{distance metric}
that makes paired anchor features as close as possible in a shared geometric
space. This is conceptually distinct from CCA+Affine, which projects both
spaces into a subspace and then learns a translation. RML instead deforms the
measurement geometry of the feature space so that 3D-text paired samples
are intrinsically nearby.

\subsection{Implementation}
\label{subsec:rml_implementation}

RML learns a Mahalanobis distance matrix $M = L^\top L$ (symmetric positive
definite, SPD) that minimises the pairwise distances between anchor pairs:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}(M) = \sum_{i=1}^{n_A} \|L(x_i - y_i)\|^2
    \label{eq:rml_loss}
\end{equation}
where $x_i$ and $y_i$ are the CCA-projected 3D and text features of the
$i$-th anchor pair, and $L$ is the Cholesky factor of $M$, ensuring positive
definiteness throughout optimization.

The matrix $L$ is optimized via stochastic gradient descent directly on the
Cholesky factor, with batch size 256, learning rate 0.01, and 200 iterations.
During retrieval, cosine similarity is replaced by the learned Mahalanobis
distance:
\begin{equation}
    \text{sim}_M(x, y) = -\|L(x - y)\|^2
    \label{eq:mahalanobis_sim}
\end{equation}
This formulation allows RML to learn an anisotropic geometry that weights
different dimensions of the CCA subspace according to their discriminative
power for cross-modal retrieval.

\subsection{Results}
\label{subsec:rml_results}

RML achieves 27.2\% Top-5 on PointNet$\times$CLIP (Table~\ref{tab:nonlinear_results}),
the strongest result among all non-linear alternatives and the closest to
CCA+Affine (35.7\%) at 76\% of its performance. Across all encoder pairs,
RML consistently outperforms KCCA by a substantial margin, suggesting that
directly minimising anchor-pair distances in a learned metric space is a more
effective strategy than discovering non-linear correlations via kernelisation.

The performance gap between RML (27.2\%) and CCA+Affine (35.7\%) likely
reflects the fundamental difference in how the two methods use supervision.
The affine map learns a global linear transformation that minimises the
reconstruction error across \emph{all} anchor pairs simultaneously, producing
a coherent global alignment. RML, by contrast, minimises local pairwise
distances, which may not generalise as effectively to unseen query pairs.

and substantially outperform unsupervised ones, and within supervised methods,
the simpler linear approach (CCA+Affine) outperforms the non-linear alternatives.
This counter-intuitive finding underscores that the performance bottleneck
in this setting is not the expressiveness of the alignment model, but rather
the availability of explicit paired supervision and the intrinsic geometric
compatibility of the latent spaces being aligned.
