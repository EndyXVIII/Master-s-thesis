\chapter{Gromov-Wasserstein Alignment and Structural Analysis}
\label{ch:gw_alignment}

The supervised \gls{cca} baseline demonstrated that a linear cross-modal mapping is achievable, but relying on large paired datasets fundamentally restricts the scalability and generalisability of this multimodal learning approach. This chapter addresses the natural follow-up question: can the 3D and textual latent spaces be aligned in a purely unsupervised or semi-supervised manner using the mathematical framework of Optimal Transport (OT)?

This distinction constitutes a central methodological premise of the present chapter. \gls{cca} + Affine, evaluated in Chapter~\ref{ch:baseline}, is a fully \emph{supervised} method: it requires $n_A = 30{,}000$ explicitly paired 3D-text anchors to learn its projection and affine mapping, and its objective is defined entirely in terms of minimising error on these known correspondences. Gromov-Wasserstein, by contrast, is --- in its base formulation --- a fully \emph{unsupervised} method: it receives no paired correspondences whatsoever, relying instead on the intra-modal relational structure of each space independently to recover an alignment. The semi-supervised \gls{gw} variants introduced later in this chapter (SS-RISGW, FGW) occupy an intermediate position, employing a substantially smaller set of anchor pairs (1,000, as opposed to \gls{cca}'s 30,000) as a weak steering signal rather than as the sole basis for alignment. This supervision asymmetry is fundamental to the interpretation of subsequent results: any comparison between \gls{cca}+Affine and the \gls{gw} family constitutes, by construction, a comparison between a supervised upper bound and unsupervised or weakly-supervised alternatives, rather than an evaluation of methods operating under equivalent informational conditions.

Standard \gls{ot} methods --- the classic Wasserstein distance, for instance --- require both distributions to reside in the same metric space to compute a direct transport cost. The 3D geometries and text captions utilized in this study inhabit completely disjoint, heterogeneous dimensional spaces. Therefore, the alignment is framed as a metric measure problem using the Gromov-Wasserstein ($\mathcal{GW}$) distance, which compares intra-modal topological structure --- essentially the pairwise distance matrices of the two spaces --- rather than comparing feature coordinates directly. Translating this elegant theoretical framework into a computationally viable implementation requires navigating significant computational bottlenecks and strict geometric assumptions. This chapter details the implementation of scalable $\mathcal{GW}$ variants, documents a comprehensive ablation study, and delineates precisely where the structural isometry assumption breaks down for real-world neural representations.

\section{Methods and Implementation}
\label{sec:gw_methods}

The standard $\mathcal{GW}$ optimisation problem is computationally demanding. Solving for the exact dense coupling matrix $\pi$ scales at $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ with the number of samples, and the memory cost of storing the cost matrices scales at $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$. Given the objective to align tens of thousands of high-dimensional embeddings from Objaverse, computing the exact transport plan is computationally intractable --- even on the University of Padova's \gls{hpc} infrastructure. To render the alignment tractable, we implemented several advanced relaxations and hardware-accelerated \gls{ot} solvers.

\subsection{Sliced GW and Rotation-Invariant Variants}
\label{subsec:sliced_gw}

A persistent challenge when processing diverse 3D objects from a crowdsourced dataset like Objaverse is the lack of canonical orientation. A vehicle may be aligned along the positive Z-axis in one mesh and the negative X-axis in another. Unless the geometric encoder guarantees rotation invariance, these arbitrary orientations introduce distortions directly into the intra-modal distance matrices, artificially inflating the apparent structural gap between the 3D and text manifolds.

To address this without a computationally expensive pre-alignment step, we implemented rotation-invariant formulations of the Gromov-Wasserstein problem. Following Algorithm 1 proposed by \textcite{vayer2019optimal}, we utilized Sliced Gromov-Wasserstein (SGW) approximations: for each of 100 random unit vectors $\theta \in S^{k-1}$, both spaces are projected onto $\theta$, the projections are sorted, and the ordering --- identity or anti-identity --- that minimises the 1D \gls{gw} cost receives a vote in a running score matrix. This configuration operates entirely without supervision. Because 1D optimal transport plans can be computed in $\mathcal{O}(n \log n)$ time via simple sorting, integrating these costs over hundreds of random projections provided a robust approximation of the true $\mathcal{GW}$ distance, enabling aggressive scaling of batch sizes during early exploratory runs without exceeding the memory limits of our NVIDIA A100 GPUs.

The Rotation-Invariant SGW (RISGW) variant extends this by explicitly optimising a rotation matrix $\Delta$ on the Stiefel manifold (via Pymanopt and \gls{jax} autodiff) to minimise $\text{SGW}_\Delta(X, Y)$, utilizing 20 projection directions and 30 gradient descent iterations, again without supervision. A Semi-Supervised extension (SS-RISGW) incorporates an anchor supervision penalty term, $\text{loss}(\Delta) = \text{SGW}_\Delta + \lambda \|\Delta X_{\text{anc}} - Y_{\text{anc}}\|^2$, weighted by $\lambda \in \{0, 0.1, 1.0, 10.0\}$, to guide the rotation toward known correspondences. This constitutes the only \gls{gw} variant tested that explicitly incorporates anchor labels into its objective.

\subsection{Fused GW and Anchor-Constrained FGW}
\label{subsec:fused_gw}

It is important to note that the anchor and sample counts used for the unsupervised and semi-supervised GW variants (Table~\ref{tab:gw_results}) are deliberately smaller than the $n_A = 30{,}000$ anchors used for \gls{cca}+Affine. This is a strict computational necessity. \gls{fgw} requires a dense $n \times n$ feature-distance cost matrix; at $n = 30{,}000$, this demands approximately 3.6\,GB of memory and becomes prohibitively slow to compute, even with GPU acceleration. \gls{sgw} is substantially more efficient ($\mathcal{O}(Ln\log n)$ for $L$ projections), yet an exhaustive grid search across anchor counts $n \in \{1\text{k}, 5\text{k}, 30\text{k}\}$ (detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:appendix_a}) yielded no systematic improvement past $n=5{,}000$ samples. This indicates that the bottleneck is structural, rooted in the absence of exploitable isometry, rather than a consequence of data scarcity.

Pure $\mathcal{GW}$ relies exclusively on topological structure --- the relational distance matrices $C_X$ and $C_Y$ --- but Fused Gromov-Wasserstein (FGW) \cite{vayer2019optimal}, implemented here via the \gls{pot} library, incorporates a mechanism to combine structural matching with direct feature matching. The \gls{fgw} formulation employs a hyperparameter $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ to modulate the trade-off between the Wasserstein cost (feature alignment) and the Gromov-Wasserstein cost (structural alignment):
\begin{equation}
\text{FGW}(X, Y) = (1 - \alpha)\,W(X, Y) + \alpha\,\mathcal{GW}(X, Y)
\label{eq:fgw}
\end{equation}

Because the modalities occupy disjoint spaces, a direct feature cost is only mathematically valid once both are projected into a shared \gls{cca} subspace. We executed \gls{fgw} within the $k=50$ \gls{cca} subspace, sweeping $\alpha$ from 0 to 1, identifying the optimal configuration at $\alpha = 0.2$ utilizing 1,000 anchor pairs.

Additionally, an Anchor-Constrained \gls{fgw} variant was evaluated. This approach incorporated anchor and query points simultaneously (1,500 total samples), explicitly setting the feature-distance matrix to zero cost on the known anchor pairs $(i,i)$, and imposing a penalty on off-diagonal anchor entries (penalty values $\in \{0, 10, 100\}$). The objective was to utilize known correspondences as fixed structural scaffolding to guide the solver. Both configurations yielded identical results to standard \gls{fgw}, achieving $4.4\%$ Top-5 accuracy, suggesting that the structural incompatibility is too severe for even locally supervised anchoring to overcome.

\subsection{Low-Rank GW via OTT-JAX}
\label{subsec:low_rank_gw}

For the definitive, large-scale unsupervised experiments, we migrated the pipeline to the OTT-JAX library, built specifically on the \gls{jax} framework to support hardware-accelerated, auto-differentiable optimal transport, facilitating full utilization of the Slurm cluster's parallelisation capabilities.

Instead of computing the full, dense transport plan $\pi \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, we utilized the library's Low-Rank Gromov-Wasserstein solver. Employing LRSinkhorn as the linear sub-solver, the coupling matrix was factorised as $T \approx Q\,\text{diag}(1/g)\,R^\top$ with a predefined rank $r$ (where $r \ll n$). The optimisation used an angular cost function, structurally mirroring Eq.~3 by \textcite{li2026scale}. Factorising the transport plan reduced spatial complexity from quadratic to linear, allowing the processing of substantially larger subsets of the 46,675 paired samples. Furthermore, this factorisation served as an implicit regulariser: restricting the rank forces the solver to focus on dominant, macro-level topological clusters, effectively filtering out high-frequency noise and minor geometric anomalies within the raw 3D mesh encodings.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:gw_results}

Evaluating the efficacy of the Gromov-Wasserstein solvers requires a direct comparison against the supervised upper bound established in Chapter~\ref{ch:baseline}. To ensure strict comparability, all \gls{ot}-based matching and retrieval metrics were computed on the identical held-out query set of 500 pairs, utilizing distance representations extracted from the PointNet and \gls{clip} (ViT-bigG-14) backbones.

The quantitative results reveal a stark performance gap between the theoretical elegance of optimal transport and its practical application on complex neural representations. This section first evaluates the purely unsupervised solvers, followed by an analysis of the semi-supervised anchoring experiments.

\subsection{Quantitative Evaluation of Unsupervised GW}
\label{subsec:unsupervised_gw_results}

The primary objective was true zero-shot alignment utilizing the Sliced Gromov-Wasserstein (SGW) and Low-Rank Gromov-Wasserstein (LR-GW) implementations. Both algorithms were tasked with constructing a coupling matrix $\pi$ utilizing exclusively the intra-modal pairwise distance matrices $C_X$ and $C_Y$, completely devoid of cross-modal anchor points to guide the optimisation. Table~\ref{tab:gw_results} details the corresponding retrieval accuracies.

It is important to note that the ``$n$'' column specifies the number of samples used to estimate the intra-domain distance matrices $C_X$ and $C_Y$ (or, for semi-supervised variants, the number of anchor pairs utilized in the supervised penalty term). For fully unsupervised methods ($\times$), this does \emph{not} imply paired cross-modal supervision: the samples are drawn independently from each modality and no ground-truth correspondence informs the optimisation.

Every purely unsupervised method failed to approach the linear baseline. In several instances, exact matching accuracy barely exceeded random chance. Rather than dismissing this as a simple algorithmic failure, an examination of the transport plan itself proves highly revealing regarding the underlying latent space topologies.

While the \gls{cca} projection explicitly aligns the axes of maximum variance based on known semantic pairs, the \gls{gw} solver lacks this guidance. It operates under the assumption of geometric homology, attempting to superimpose the 3D distance manifold directly onto the text distance manifold. The severe degradation in Top-$K$ retrieval indicates a critical limitation: minimising the global metric measure discrepancy (the \gls{gw} objective) does not correlate with minimising semantic discrepancy. In practice, the optimiser consistently collapsed into localised topological traps. Densely populated categories in Objaverse --- such as ``furniture'' or ``vehicles'' --- were mapped en masse onto entirely unrelated but similarly dense clusters within the text space. The algorithm successfully matched statistical \textit{density} and \textit{variance}, but it completely scrambled semantic \textit{identities}.

Furthermore, the low-rank constraint within OTT-JAX, while computationally mandatory to prevent memory exhaustion on the \gls{hpc} cluster, inherently forces the coupling matrix to smooth out high-frequency matching details. While this smoothing stabilizes gradients during optimisation, it compromises exact instance-level retrieval. It frequently produces ``many-to-one'' assignments, collapsing numerous distinct 3D queries onto a single, centrally located text embedding.

\subsection{The Impact of Semi-Supervised Anchoring (FGW)}
\label{subsec:fgw_results}

Given the failure of the purely unsupervised approach, the subsequent investigation focused on whether injecting a limited supervised signal into the transport plan could improve alignment. Fused Gromov-Wasserstein (FGW) achieves this by combining a direct feature-distance term with the structural \gls{gw} term, modulated by the trade-off parameter $\alpha$. An $\alpha = 0.2$ configuration utilizing 1,000 anchor pairs was evaluated, deliberately weighting the Wasserstein (feature-distance) term heavier than the structural \gls{gw} term.

Additionally, an Anchor-Constrained \gls{fgw} variant was tested. This approach set the cost to zero on the 1,000 known anchor pairs within the feature-distance matrix and applied an additional penalty on off-diagonal anchor entries (penalty $\in \{0, 10, 100\}$). This effectively treated known correspondences as immutable structural anchor points around which the unsupervised assignments were forced to optimize.

Both configurations yielded identical results: $4.4\%$ Top-5 accuracy, directly matching the standard \gls{fgw} performance (Table~\ref{tab:gw_results}). The anchor constraints failed to propagate any additional alignment signal through the broader geometric structure. This strongly indicates that the structural incompatibility between the two latent spaces is too profound for even locally supervised anchoring to mitigate --- a hypothesis rigorously examined in the ablation study of Section~\ref{sec:ablation_study}.

\section{Ablation Study --- Thirteen Diagnostic Tests}
\label{sec:ablation_study}

The severe degradation in retrieval accuracy observed across the unsupervised experiments necessitated a rigorous diagnostic phase. When an optimisation algorithm fails to converge on a meaningful semantic mapping, the failure typically originates from one of three sources: implementation errors, unstable hyperparameter configurations, or fundamental incompatibilities in the underlying mathematical assumptions of the data itself. To isolate the cause, an exhaustive ablation study spanning thirteen distinct experimental configurations was executed. This systematically challenged every variable in the pipeline, ensuring that the observed \gls{gw} performance degradation genuinely reflected latent space topologies rather than technical oversights. Tests 1 through 11 cover standard algorithmic variables, while Tests 12 and 13 address advanced methodological questions regarding feature preprocessing and entropic smoothing.

\subsection{Implementation Validation (Tests 1 and 2)}
\label{subsec:implementation_validation}

Prior to questioning the structural integrity of the Objaverse dataset itself, it was imperative to validate the structural soundness of the custom Optimal Transport solvers, particularly the Low-Rank Gromov-Wasserstein (LR-GW) implementation via OTT-JAX.

\paragraph{Test 1: Synthetic Sanity Check.}
Synthetic scenarios were constructed to guarantee the existence of a correct mathematical alignment. A target space $Y = XQ$ was defined, with $Q$ functioning as a random orthogonal matrix representing a perfect geometric isometry (a rotation in high-dimensional space). Operating with $n=500$, $d=50$, a rank constraint of 200, and $\epsilon=0.1$, the \gls{lrgw} solver recovered the mapping perfectly, achieving $93.4\%$ Top-5 accuracy on the rotated data, and $91.6\%$ on reflected data ($Y = X$ with a sign flip).

Notably, Case C --- a rotation corrupted by moderate Gaussian noise, $Y = XQ + \mathcal{N}(0, 0.1)$ --- collapsed to near-random performance ($1.8\%$ Top-5), despite the underlying isometric structure remaining technically present. This indicates that the \gls{lrgw} solver is highly sensitive to even modest perturbations of an otherwise perfectly isometric mapping, and does not solely fail in the total absence of structure. When mapping onto pure independent Gaussian noise (Case D), performance degraded to the expected random-guessing baseline of $1.6\%$.

Taken together, Cases C and D demonstrate that the solver's capacity to recover isometric structure is highly fragile. This raises the critical question of whether the failure observed on real 3D-text data (Section~\ref{sec:gw_results}) reflects a genuine absence of isometry, or simply a substantial degree of non-isometric noise obscuring a weaker signal. This distinction is addressed directly through the self-\gls{gw} and \gls{cka}-based analysis in Section~\ref{sec:why_gw_fails}. However, this test establishes definitively that the solver is fully capable of finding isometric alignments when they are cleanly presented (Cases A--B).

\paragraph{Test 2: Self-GW on Real Features.}
To exclude potential numerical anomalies within the Objaverse features, the solver was tasked with aligning a modality with itself. Utilizing \gls{cca}-projected features ($k=50$, consistent with the main protocol) at rank $r=200$ --- mirroring the solver settings of Test 1 --- the $\mathcal{GW}$ distance was computed between the PointNet features and themselves, and equivalently for \gls{clip}. The algorithm achieved $86.6\%$ Top-5 accuracy for self-aligned PointNet and $88.0\%$ for self-aligned \gls{clip}. The structural cost of aligning the two distinct spaces (PointNet to CLIP, $0.0332$) was measured at $1.4\times$ higher than aligning identical spaces. This aligns well with the post-\gls{cca} \gls{gw} cost reported for this pairing in the diagnostic analysis of Section~\ref{sec:why_gw_fails} (Table~\ref{tab:gw-diagnostic}, $0.031$); the marginal variance reflects the specific sample subset utilized for this rank-200 sanity check versus the comprehensive diagnostic sweep. This confirms that the implementation is robust; the alignment failure stems from the cross-modal data topology itself, rather than programmatic errors.

\subsection{Hyperparameter and Metric Sweeps (Tests 3 to 6)}
\label{subsec:hyperparameter_sweeps}

Having validated the codebase, the investigation shifted to the optimisation dynamics. The $\mathcal{GW}$ loss landscape is highly non-convex, and gradient-based solvers are susceptible to stagnation within localised topological traps if regularisation is improperly tuned.

\paragraph{Test 3: Epsilon Regularisation Sweep.}
Entropic regularisation ($\epsilon$) governs the smoothness of the transport plan. Eight values were swept ($\epsilon \in \{0.001, 0.005, 0.01, 0.05,\allowbreak 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 5.0\}$) at rank 20 employing a cosine cost. The solver returned an identical final \gls{gw} cost of $0.0392$ across all eight configurations. The algorithm consistently converges to the exact same stationary point regardless of regularisation strength, indicating that no superior local minimum exists within the landscape for a divergent $\epsilon$ to discover.

\paragraph{Test 4: Cost Function Formulation.}
High-dimensional neural representations do not always conform intuitively to standard Euclidean norms. The default squared-Euclidean distance was replaced with an angular distance metric $d(x, x') = \arccos\!\left(\tfrac{x \cdot x'}{\|x\|\,\|x'\|}\right)$, conforming to the methodology proposed by \textcite{li2026scale}. This angular metric increased Top-5 accuracy from $0.4\%$ to $1.9\%$. While a measurable improvement, it remains approximately $18\times$ below the supervised \gls{cca}+Affine baseline \cite{hadgi2025escaping}, indicating that the distance function formulation is not the primary limiting factor.

\paragraph{Test 5: Raw Features and Scale-Matching.}
To determine if normalisation artifacts were responsible, \gls{lrgw} was executed directly on raw, pre-\gls{cca} normalised features. The median-ratio scale correction introduced by \textcite{li2026scale}, $s = \text{med}(D_Y)/\text{med}(D_X)$, was applied to adjust for disparities in modal density. The resulting performance --- $0.5\%$ Top-5 accuracy, irrespective of correction --- demonstrates that simple scale discrepancies are not the primary issue.

\paragraph{Test 6: Rank Constraint Sweep.}
The rank parameter was aggressively swept across seven values: $r \in \{2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200\}$. Performance peaked at rank 50, achieving $2.4\%$ Top-5 accuracy. By contrast, in the synthetic Test 1, increasing the rank from 20 to 200 dramatically improved Case A from $19\%$ to $93\%$; no comparable improvement manifested on the empirical dataset at any rank. The performance bottleneck is therefore not bounded by the model's representational capacity, but by the structural incompatibility of the data.

\subsection{Dataset and Architecture Generalisation (Tests 7 to 9)}
\label{subsec:dataset_generalization}

Subsequent tests evaluated whether specific dataset sampling constraints or architectural choices induced the alignment collapse.

\paragraph{Test 7: Query Set Expansion.}
To assess if a 500-pair query set lacked the density required to properly orient the manifolds, query set sizes of $n \in \{500, 1000, 2000\}$ were evaluated. Performance did not improve with augmented data --- degrading slightly at $n=2000$ --- confirming that data scarcity is not the limiting factor.

\paragraph{Test 8: CCA Dimension Sweep.}
To determine if the $k=50$ \gls{cca} subspace discarded excessive structural variance, subspace dimensions of $k \in \{10, 20, 50, 100\}$ were evaluated. \gls{lrgw} achieved its peak performance at $k=20$ ($3.0\%$ Top-5), compared to $30.1\%$ for the supervised \gls{cca}+Affine baseline within the identical $k=20$ subspace. The substantial performance gap remains consistent across all evaluated dimensionalities.

\paragraph{Test 9: Comprehensive Encoder Matrix.}
To exclude topological artifacts specific to PointNet or \gls{clip}, the standard \gls{lrgw} configuration (rank$=20$, $\epsilon=0.1$, 3 seeds) was evaluated across all six encoder permutations --- PointNet and SparseConv paired against \gls{clip}, \gls{roberta}, and \gls{bert}. The unsupervised retrieval metrics remained flat, fluctuating narrowly between $1.1\%$ and $1.7\%$ Top-5 accuracy regardless of the paired encoders. The supervised \gls{cca}+Affine baseline, conversely, exhibits significant variance across the identical six pairs, ranging from $10.2\%$ to $34.1\%$ Top-5, accurately reflecting variations in encoder quality. The \gls{lrgw} framework proved completely blind to these differences, definitively excluding encoder-specific anomalies and confirming that the \gls{gw} failure is intrinsic to cross-modal alignment methodologies.

\paragraph{Test 11: Transport Plan Inspection.}
An examination of the raw structure of the transport matrix $T$ was conducted. An effective retrieval matrix concentrates probability mass heavily along its diagonal. Analysis revealed that the \gls{lrgw} transport plan's diagonal-to-uniform ratio ranged from $0.4\times$ to $1.2\times$. Conversely, the pseudo-transport matrix induced by the supervised \gls{cca}+Affine mapping --- generated by shifting the cosine-similarity matrix to non-negative values and renormalising to unity --- exhibited a diagonal-to-uniform ratio of $1.6\times$, significantly exceeding the uniform baseline of $1.0\times$.

It is necessary to contextualize this comparison: the raw entropy of this \gls{cca}+Affine pseudo-plan is not directly comparable to a genuine transport plan. Cosine similarities between normalised embeddings fluctuate within a narrow numerical band across the \emph{entire} $n \times n$ matrix. Consequently, the resulting pseudo-plan appears globally near-flat in absolute value (entropy $\approx 12.4$, approaching the theoretical maximum $\log(n^2)$), despite the \emph{relative ranking} within each row correctly favoring the diagonal element. The \gls{lrgw} plan, however, constitutes a genuine Sinkhorn-derived coupling with substantially lower absolute entropy ($7.4$--$9.6$), reflecting concentrated probability mass --- yet concentrated on the \emph{incorrect} cells, consistent with the aforementioned many-to-one collapse. Thus, the diagonal-to-uniform ratio serves as the most informative and directly comparable statistic between the two methods, as it is normalised against the identical $n^2$-cell baseline irrespective of overall matrix shape. By this metric, the supervised baseline exhibits genuine structural concentration, whereas the unsupervised \gls{lrgw} solver fails to do so.

\section{Theoretical Analysis: Why GW Cannot Work}
\label{sec:why_gw_fails}

The ablation study in Section~\ref{sec:ablation_study} systematically eliminated every conventional point of computational failure. With implementation, hyperparameters, and libraries all validated, the persistent inability of Gromov-Wasserstein to align 3D objects with textual descriptions must trace back to a fundamental violation of the mathematical assumptions underlying the Optimal Transport framework itself.

This section formulates the theoretical reasons behind that collapse, setting the intrinsic topological mismatch of the latent spaces against the success of the supervised linear baseline.

\subsection{The Absence of Structural Isometry}
\label{subsec:isometry_bottleneck}

The theoretical justification for utilizing the Gromov-Wasserstein distance relies on its capacity to bypass heterogeneous coordinate systems by matching relational intra-modal distance matrices. However, a foundational theorem by \textcite{vayer2019optimal} proves that the \gls{gw} distance between two spaces converges to a meaningful zero-cost minimum \textit{if and only if} those spaces are globally isometric --- meaning there exists a rigorous, distance-preserving bijection connecting their structures.

The empirical results clearly demonstrate that the PointNet and \gls{clip} latent spaces do not share an isometric topology. \textcite{hadgi2025escaping} theoretically corroborate this: they evaluated structural similarity across several 3D-text encoder combinations using Linear Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA, their Figure 2). The contrast is stark: OpenShape, a 3D encoder trained \emph{jointly} with text supervision, achieves a CKA of $0.31$ with CLIP --- the highest score in their entire comparison, though still below the $1.0$ required for true isometry. Conversely, PointNet, trained entirely independently of any text signal, manages only $0.12$ with the identical CLIP text encoder, and every combination involving RoBERTa or BERT scores substantially lower ($0.01$--$0.04$). This roughly $2.5\times$ variance between jointly-trained and independently-trained encoders exemplifies the core pattern of this thesis: encoders utilizing uncoupled training objectives exhibit substantially less shared structure than those explicitly trained to align.

Applying the methodology proposed by \textcite{li2026scale} --- who utilize \gls{gw} as a training-free thermometer for latent space compatibility rather than an alignment tool --- the evaluated encoders consistently converged to a \gls{gw} cost of approximately $0.031$ to $0.032$ post-CCA, at rank $r=200$ (matching the self-GW validation protocol of Test 2, Section~\ref{subsec:implementation_validation}). This should not be conflated with the \gls{gw} cost of $0.0392$ reported in Tests 3 and 13 (Sections~\ref{subsec:hyperparameter_sweeps} and \ref{subsec:advanced_verifications}); that value was computed at the lower rank of $r=20$ used for entropic regularisation sweeps, where lower-rank solvers systematically settle on higher residual costs due to coarser transport plan approximation. Table~\ref{tab:gw-diagnostic} details this diagnostic breakdown across all six encoder pairs, revealing a striking pattern: raw, pre-normalisation metric scales are wildly incompatible --- with scale factors ranging from $s \approx 0.09$ up to $s \approx 228{,}000$ --- yet every pair converges to essentially the same near-uninformative \gls{gw} cost ($\approx 0.031$--$0.032$) once projected into the shared \gls{cca} subspace.

Devoid of underlying structural homology, the \gls{gw} solver lacks geometric anchors --- resulting in a transport plan that is mathematically valid, but semantically meaningless.

\subsection{External Verification: Blind Matching with an Exact QAP Solver}
\label{subsec:blind_match_verification}

The thirteen-test ablation study detailed in Section~\ref{sec:ablation_study} systematically eliminated implementation and hyperparameter inconsistencies as the source of the \gls{gw} failure, isolating structural non-isometry as the definitive root cause. As a final external validation of this conclusion, we investigated whether this collapse was specific to our chosen \gls{gw} solver or indicative of a more fundamental limitation in unsupervised structural matching between these spaces. To evaluate this, a secondary, entirely independent solver was applied to the dataset: the factorized Hahn-Grant solver introduced by \textcite{schnaus2025blind}. This exact quadratic assignment problem (\gls{qap}) solver frames blind vision-language matching as a structural optimisation problem highly analogous to the \gls{gw} objective. Both methods aim to recover a correspondence that maximally preserves intra-domain relational structure; however, the Hahn-Grant solver utilizes exact dual ascent over discrete permutations rather than approximating a continuous transport plan via entropic Sinkhorn regularisation.

\paragraph{Setup.} Object-level matching, as employed throughout this thesis, possesses a granularity far exceeding the capacity of an exact \gls{qap} solver. The factorized Hahn-Grant method scales at $\mathcal{O}(N^3)$ in memory, and the original authors confirm the global optimum is provably recoverable only up to $N \approx 40$ items. To ensure a valid comparison, the 46,675 paired objects were aggregated into semantic categories, extracted automatically from their Cap3D captions via head-noun part-of-speech tagging (excluding generic filler terminology such as ``object'' or ``structure'' to prevent uninformative category dominance). This produced category-level PointNet and \gls{clip} embeddings --- defined as one mean feature vector per category, per modality --- strictly adhering to the aggregation protocol established in the original Blind Match paper. Two problem sizes were evaluated: $N=10$, incorporating the ten most densely populated and semantically unambiguous categories (\textit{house, building, character, sculpture, block, head, rock, chair, roof, table}), and $N=46$, incorporating every category retaining a minimum of 20 associated objects post-filtering.

Accuracy fell to $4.3\%$, marginally exceeding the $2.2\%$ random baseline (a $2\times$ ratio, compared to $6\times$ at $N=10$). Analysis of the resulting assignment revealed that the majority of the 46 categories were matched to semantically unrelated partners (e.g., \textit{sword} matched to \textit{teapot}, \textit{man} matched to \textit{skull}, and \textit{house} matched to \textit{building} --- a near-miss but definitively incorrect). \paragraph{Interpretation.} These results reinforce the central finding of this chapter. They confirm that the alignment failure is not an artefact localized to the specific \gls{gw} solver utilized throughout Chapter~\ref{ch:gw_alignment}. An entirely distinct, exact optimisation method, implemented by an independent research group to address an analogous matching problem, exhibits an identical qualitative pattern: non-trivial structure is recoverable at a small, aggregated scale, but degrades to chance performance as the problem dimensionality expands. The two parameters that render the $N=10$ configuration tractable --- heavy aggregation across hundreds of objects per category, and the deployment of an exact rather than approximate solver --- are precisely the parameters absent from the primary object-level retrieval task. The primary task demands the matching of 500 individual query objects, each possessing idiosyncratic geometry and captioning, without the benefit of aggregation to smooth noise, and necessitating a solver capable of scaling to tens of thousands of anchor points. Category aggregation inherently filters the instance-level noise that the thirteen-test ablation study (Section~\ref{sec:ablation_study}) identified as fatal to unsupervised \gls{gw} at the object level; removing that aggregation forces both solvers to converge on the identical conclusion.

\paragraph{Why does aggregation recover so much accuracy?} The jump from near-random performance at the object level (Section~\ref{sec:gw_results}) to $60\%$ accuracy at $N=10$ merits explicit justification rather than being accepted at face value. Three factors combine to produce it. First, averaging feature vectors across hundreds of objects per category suppresses instance-level noise, leaving behind something closer to a category prototype --- the geometric and linguistic regularities that render a chair recognisably a chair, stripped of the idiosyncratic variation any single chair introduces on its own. This is precisely the kind of noise Test 1 (Section~\ref{subsec:implementation_validation}) already demonstrated to be fatal to \gls{gw}: Case C there --- a perfect isometry corrupted by only moderate Gaussian noise --- collapsed from $93.4\%$ to $1.8\%$ Top-5, confirming that unsupervised solvers are highly sensitive to exactly this type of per-sample perturbation, which category aggregation removes almost entirely. Second, ten semantically distant categories --- a house, a rifle, a sculpture --- are plausibly separated by a coarse relational structure that survives even where fine-grained, object-level isometry does not: if ``house'' and ``building'' occupy nearby positions in both the 3D and text distance matrices while ``rifle'' sits far from both, that coarse ordering can persist even when the detailed internal geometry of each category is not preserved across modalities. Third, the Hahn-Grant solver is exact rather than approximate: at $N=10$ the permutation space is small enough ($10! \approx 3.6$ million) for the solver to certify the global optimum within seconds, extracting the entirety of whatever weak structure is present --- unlike the approximate \gls{lrgw} solver, which Test 11 (Section~\ref{subsec:library_analysis}) demonstrated can stall in uninformative local minima (diagonal ratio $0.4$--$1.2\times$) even when some structure exists.

The result is also unlikely to constitute a statistical artefact: under a uniform-random permutation of ten items, the expected number of correct matches is exactly one, not six, rendering the observed outcome --- reproducibly certified as the global optimum, rather than a single fortunate run --- a genuinely improbable event under the null hypothesis of no underlying structure whatsoever. Crucially, the two conditions rendering $N=10$ tractable --- heavy category-level aggregation and an exact solver --- are precisely the two conditions absent from the primary retrieval task investigated throughout this thesis, and the collapse to $4.3\%$ at $N=46$ (still employing aggregated categories and the identical exact solver) confirms that this coarse relational signal itself begins to dissipate well before approaching the scale and granularity of realistic object-level retrieval.
