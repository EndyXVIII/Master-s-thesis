\chapter{Gromov-Wasserstein Alignment}
\label{ch:gw_alignment}

The supervised \gls{cca} baseline showed that a linear cross-modal mapping is achievable, but leaning on large paired datasets fundamentally caps how scalable and generalisable that kind of multimodal learning can be. This chapter asks the natural follow-up question: can the 3D and textual latent spaces be aligned in a purely unsupervised or semi-supervised way instead, using the mathematical machinery of Optimal Transport (OT)?

Standard \gls{ot} methods --- the classic Wasserstein distance, for instance --- need both distributions to live in the same metric space to compute a direct transport cost. Our 3D geometries and text captions don't cooperate here: they inhabit completely disjoint, heterogeneous dimensional spaces. So we frame the alignment as a metric measure problem instead, using the Gromov-Wasserstein ($\mathcal{GW}$) distance, which compares intra-modal topological structure --- essentially the pairwise distance matrices of the two spaces --- rather than feature coordinates directly. Getting from this elegant mathematical theory to something that actually runs, though, means navigating some serious computational bottlenecks and fairly strict geometric assumptions. This chapter works through our implementation of scalable $\mathcal{GW}$ variants, documents a comprehensive ablation study, and ultimately lays bare exactly where the structural isometry assumption breaks down for real-world neural representations.

\section{Methods and Implementation}
\label{sec:gw_methods}

The standard $\mathcal{GW}$ optimisation problem is expensive by nature. Solving for the exact dense coupling matrix $\pi$ scales at $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ with the number of samples, and the memory cost of storing the cost matrices scales at $\mathcal{O}(n^2)$. Given that we're trying to align tens of thousands of high-dimensional embeddings from Objaverse, computing the exact transport plan simply isn't feasible --- not even on the University of Padova's HPC infrastructure. To make the alignment tractable at all, we implemented several advanced relaxations and hardware-accelerated \gls{ot} solvers.

\subsection{Sliced GW and Rotation-Invariant Variants}
\label{subsec:sliced_gw}

One persistent headache when working with diverse 3D objects from a crowdsourced dataset like Objaverse is that there's no canonical orientation. A vehicle might sit along the positive Z-axis in one mesh and the negative X-axis in another, with nothing to normalise that away. If the geometric encoder doesn't guarantee rotation invariance on its own, these arbitrary orientations bleed directly into distorted intra-modal distance matrices, inflating the apparent structural gap between the 3D and text manifolds artificially.

To handle this without a computationally heavy pre-alignment step, we brought in rotation-invariant formulations of the Gromov-Wasserstein problem. Following Algorithm 1 of Vayer et al.~\cite{vayer2019optimal}, we used Sliced Gromov-Wasserstein (SGW) approximations: for each of 100 random unit vectors $\theta \in S^{k-1}$, both spaces get projected onto $\theta$, the projections are sorted, and whichever ordering --- identity or anti-identity --- minimises the 1D \gls{gw} cost gets a vote in a running score matrix. No supervision anywhere in this configuration. Because 1D optimal transport plans can be computed in $\mathcal{O}(n \log n)$ time via simple sorting, integrating these costs over hundreds of random projections gave us a solid approximation of the true $\mathcal{GW}$ distance, which let us scale batch sizes fairly aggressively during early exploratory runs without running into memory limits on our NVIDIA A100 GPUs.

The Rotation-Invariant SGW (RISGW) variant builds on this by explicitly optimising a rotation matrix $\Delta$ on the Stiefel manifold (via Pymanopt and \gls{jax} autodiff) to minimise $\text{SGW}_\Delta(X, Y)$, using 20 projection directions and 30 gradient descent iterations, again with no supervision. A Semi-Supervised extension (SS-RISGW) adds an anchor supervision penalty term, $\text{loss}(\Delta) = \text{SGW}_\Delta + \lambda \|\Delta X_{\text{anc}} - Y_{\text{anc}}\|^2$, weighted by $\lambda \in \{0, 0.1, 1.0, 10.0\}$, to gently steer the rotation toward known correspondences --- this is the only \gls{gw} variant tested that explicitly uses anchor labels in its objective.

\subsection{Fused GW and Anchor-Constrained FGW}
\label{subsec:fused_gw}

Worth flagging up front: the anchor and sample counts used for the unsupervised and semi-supervised GW variants (Table~\ref{tab:gw_results}) are deliberately smaller than the $n_A = 30{,}000$ anchors used for CCA+Affine. That's a computational necessity, not an arbitrary choice. FGW needs a dense $n \times n$ feature-distance cost matrix, and at $n = 30{,}000$ that's roughly 3.6\,GB of memory and prohibitively slow to compute, even on GPU. SGW is much cheaper ($\mathcal{O}(Ln\log n)$ for $L$ projections), but an exhaustive grid search across anchor counts $n \in \{1\text{k}, 5\text{k}, 30\text{k}\}$ (details in Appendix~\ref{app:appendix_a}) turned up no systematic improvement past $n=5{,}000$ samples --- which tells us the bottleneck here is structural, rooted in the absence of exploitable isometry, and not simply a consequence of using fewer anchors.

Pure $\mathcal{GW}$ relies exclusively on topological structure --- the relational distance matrices $C_X$ and $C_Y$ --- but Fused Gromov-Wasserstein (FGW) \cite{vayer2019optimal}, implemented here via the \gls{pot} library, adds a mechanism for combining structural matching with direct feature matching. The \gls{fgw} formulation uses a hyperparameter $\alpha \in [0, 1]$ to control the trade-off between the Wasserstein cost (feature alignment) and the Gromov-Wasserstein cost (structural alignment):
\begin{equation}
    \text{FGW}(X, Y) = (1 - \alpha)\,W(X, Y) + \alpha\,\mathcal{GW}(X, Y)
    \label{eq:fgw}
\end{equation}

Since our modalities occupy disjoint spaces, a direct feature cost only makes sense once both are projected into a shared \gls{cca} subspace. We ran \gls{fgw} in the $k=50$ \gls{cca} subspace, swept $\alpha$ from 0 to 1, and found the best result at $\alpha = 0.2$ with 1,000 anchor pairs.

We also tried an Anchor-Constrained \gls{fgw} variant --- extending the setup to include anchor and query points together (1,500 total samples), setting the feature-distance matrix to zero cost on the known anchor pairs $(i,i)$, and adding a penalty on off-diagonal anchor entries (penalty values $\in \{0, 10, 100\}$). The idea was to use known correspondences as fixed structural scaffolding for the solver to build around. Both configurations landed on identical results to standard \gls{fgw}, $4.4\%$ Top-5 --- the structural incompatibility here is apparently too severe for even locally supervised anchoring to overcome.

\subsection{Low-Rank GW via OTT-JAX}
\label{subsec:low_rank_gw}

For the definitive, large-scale unsupervised experiments, and following the OTT-JAX tutorial the supervisor assigned, we moved our pipeline over to the OTT-JAX library, built specifically on top of \gls{jax} to support hardware-accelerated, auto-differentiable optimal transport --- which let us make full use of the Slurm cluster's parallelisation.

Rather than computing the full, dense transport plan $\pi \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, we used the library's Low-Rank Gromov-Wasserstein solver, with LRSinkhorn as the linear sub-solver, factorising the coupling matrix as $T \approx Q\,\text{diag}(1/g)\,R^\top$ with a user-defined rank $r$ (where $r \ll n$). We ran this with an angular cost function, matching Li et al.'s Eq.~3 \cite{li2026scale}. Factoring the transport plan cut the spatial complexity from quadratic to linear --- letting us process much larger subsets of our 46,675 paired samples at once --- and doubled as an implicit regulariser: restricting the rank forces the solver to focus on the dominant, macro-level topological clusters of the latent spaces, filtering out high-frequency noise and minor geometric quirks in the raw 3D mesh encodings along the way.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:gw_results}

Evaluating how well the Gromov-Wasserstein solvers align things means comparing them directly against the supervised upper bound established in Chapter~\ref{ch:baseline}. To keep the comparison fair, all \gls{ot}-based matching and retrieval metrics were computed on the exact same held-out query set of 500 pairs, using distance representations extracted from the PointNet and \gls{clip} (ViT-bigG-14) backbones.

A first look at the numbers reveals a stark gap between the theoretical elegance of optimal transport and how it actually performs on complex, real-world neural representations. We'll work through the purely unsupervised solvers first, then move to the semi-supervised anchoring experiments.

Every purely unsupervised method collapsed compared to the linear baseline. In several runs, exact matching accuracy barely cleared random chance. Rather than write this off as a plain algorithmic failure, it's worth digging into what the transport plan itself actually looks like --- it turns out to be quite revealing about the underlying latent space topologies.

The \gls{cca} projection forcibly aligns the axes of maximum variance based on known semantic pairs; the \gls{gw} solver has no such luxury and operates purely on geometric faith, trying to overlay the shape of the 3D distance manifold directly onto the text distance manifold. The severe drop in Top-$K$ retrieval tells us something important: minimising the global metric measure discrepancy (the \gls{gw} objective) simply doesn't track with minimising semantic discrepancy. In practice, the optimiser kept falling into localised topological traps. Densely populated categories in Objaverse --- ``furniture'' or ``vehicles,'' say --- got mapped en masse onto completely unrelated but similarly dense clusters in the text space. The algorithm was matching statistical \textit{density} and \textit{variance} just fine; it was scrambling \textit{identities} entirely.

On top of that, the low-rank constraint in OTT-JAX, while necessary to avoid running out of memory on the HPC cluster, forces the coupling matrix to smooth out high-frequency matching details. That smoothing helps stabilise gradients during optimisation, but it's genuinely bad news for exact instance-level retrieval, often producing ``many-to-one'' assignments where several distinct 3D queries all collapse onto a single, centrally located text embedding.

\subsection{The Impact of Semi-Supervised Anchoring (FGW)}
\label{subsec:fgw_results}

Given how badly the purely unsupervised approach collapsed, the obvious next question was whether injecting even a little supervised signal into the transport plan could rescue things. Fused Gromov-Wasserstein (FGW) does exactly this, combining a direct feature-distance term with the structural \gls{gw} term through the trade-off parameter $\alpha$. We tested $\alpha = 0.2$ on 1,000 anchor pairs --- a configuration that leans harder on the Wasserstein (feature-distance) term than on the structural \gls{gw} term.

We also tried an Anchor-Constrained \gls{fgw} variant, setting cost to zero on the 1,000 known anchor pairs in the feature-distance matrix, with an additional penalty on off-diagonal anchor entries (penalty $\in \{0, 10, 100\}$), treating known correspondences as immovable structural pegs around which the remaining unsupervised assignments would form.

Both configurations came out identical: 4.4\% Top-5, matching standard \gls{fgw} exactly (Table~\ref{tab:gw_results}). The anchor constraints didn't propagate any additional alignment signal through the broader geometry at all. That strongly suggests the structural incompatibility between the two spaces runs too deep for even locally supervised anchoring to fix --- a hypothesis we dig into properly in the ablation study of Section~\ref{sec:ablation_study}.

\section{Ablation Study --- Thirteen Diagnostic Tests}
\label{sec:ablation_study}

The severity of the drop in retrieval accuracy across our unsupervised experiments made a rigorous diagnostic phase unavoidable. When an optimisation algorithm fails to converge on a meaningful semantic mapping, the failure usually traces back to one of three sources: a bug somewhere in the implementation, an unstable hyperparameter configuration, or a genuine incompatibility in the underlying mathematical assumptions of the data itself. To figure out which, we designed and ran an exhaustive ablation study spanning thirteen distinct experimental configurations, deliberately challenging every variable in the pipeline in turn, so that any poor GW performance we observed could be trusted as a genuine reflection of the latent space topologies rather than a technical oversight we'd overlooked. Tests 1 through 11 cover the standard algorithmic ground; Tests 12 and 13, added on the supervisor's specific instructions, dig into advanced methodological questions around feature preprocessing and entropic smoothing.

\subsection{Implementation Validation (Tests 1 and 2)}
\label{subsec:implementation_validation}

Before questioning the geometry of the Objaverse dataset itself, we first had to be sure our custom Optimal Transport solvers --- the Low-Rank Gromov-Wasserstein (LR-GW) implementation via OTT-JAX in particular --- were structurally sound.

\paragraph{Test 1: Synthetic Sanity Check.}
We built synthetic scenarios where the correct alignment was mathematically guaranteed to exist. We defined a target space $Y = XQ$, with $Q$ a random orthogonal matrix representing a perfect geometric isometry (a rotation in high-dimensional space). Running with $n=500$, $d=50$, a rank constraint of 200, and $\epsilon=0.1$, the \gls{lrgw} solver recovered the mapping perfectly --- $93.4\%$ Top-5 accuracy on the rotated data, $91.6\%$ on reflected data ($Y = X$ with a sign flip).

Interestingly, Case C --- a rotation corrupted by moderate Gaussian noise, $Y = XQ + \mathcal{N}(0, 0.1)$ --- also collapsed to near-random performance ($1.8\%$ Top-5), even though the underlying isometric structure was still technically present. That's not just a negative control result: it tells us the \gls{lrgw} solver is quite sensitive to even modest perturbations of an otherwise perfectly isometric mapping, and doesn't only fail in the total absence of structure. When we mapped onto pure independent Gaussian noise instead (Case D), performance dropped to a random-guessing baseline of $1.6\%$, as expected.

Taken together, Cases C and D suggest the solver's ability to recover isometric structure is fairly fragile, which raises a question: could the failure on real 3D-text data (Section~\ref{sec:gw_results}) reflect not a genuine absence of isometry, but a substantial degree of non-isometric noise sitting on top of a weaker real signal? We tackle that distinction directly through the self-GW and CKA-based analysis of Section~\ref{sec:why_gw_fails}. What this test does establish definitively, though, is that the solver \emph{is} capable of finding isometric alignments when they're cleanly present (Cases A--B).

\paragraph{Test 2: Self-GW on Real Features.}
Next, to rule out any numerical quirks lurking in the Objaverse features themselves, we gave the solver the trivial task of aligning a modality with itself. Working on \gls{cca}-projected features ($k=50$, consistent with our main protocol) at rank $r=200$ --- the same solver settings as Test 1 --- we computed the $\mathcal{GW}$ distance between the PointNet features and themselves, and did the same for \gls{clip}. The algorithm hit $86.6\%$ Top-5 for self-aligned PointNet and $88.0\%$ for self-aligned \gls{clip}. The structural cost of aligning the two distinct spaces (PointNet to CLIP, $0.0332$) came out $1.4\times$ higher than aligning identical spaces, and lines up reasonably well with the post-\gls{cca} \gls{gw} cost reported for this pair in the diagnostic analysis of Section~\ref{sec:why_gw_fails} (Table~\ref{tab:gw-diagnostic}, $0.031$); the small remaining gap just reflects the specific sample subset used for this rank-200 sanity check versus the full diagnostic sweep. Between this and Test 1, the implementation checks out --- the failure is happening in the cross-modal data topology itself, not our code.

\subsection{Hyperparameter and Metric Sweeps (Tests 3 to 6)}
\label{subsec:hyperparameter_sweeps}

With the codebase validated, we turned to the optimisation dynamics themselves. The $\mathcal{GW}$ loss landscape is highly non-convex, and gradient-based solvers can get stuck in localised topological traps fairly easily if regularisation isn't tuned well.

\paragraph{Test 3: Epsilon Regularisation Sweep.}
Entropic regularisation ($\epsilon$) controls how smooth the transport plan is. We swept eight values ($\epsilon \in \{0.001, 0.005, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 5.0\}$) at rank 20 with cosine cost, and the solver returned an identical final \gls{gw} cost of $0.0392$ across all eight. In other words, the algorithm converges to exactly the same stationary point no matter how strong or weak the regularisation is --- there simply isn't a better local minimum sitting somewhere in the landscape for a different $\epsilon$ to find.

\paragraph{Test 4: Cost Function Formulation.}
High-dimensional neural representations don't always behave intuitively under standard Euclidean norms. We swapped the default squared-Euclidean distance (OTT-JAX's default) for an angular distance metric $d(x, x') = \arccos\!\left(\tfrac{x \cdot x'}{\|x\|\,\|x'\|}\right)$, following Li et al.~\cite{li2026scale}. The angular metric bumped Top-5 accuracy from $0.4\%$ to $1.9\%$ --- a real improvement, but a modest one, still roughly $18\times$ below the supervised \gls{cca}+Affine baseline \cite{hadgi2025escaping}. The distance function isn't what's holding things back.

\paragraph{Test 5: Raw Features and Scale-Matching.}
Next we checked whether normalisation was somehow the culprit. Running LR-GW directly on raw, pre-\gls{cca} normalised features, we applied Li et al.'s median-ratio scale correction $s = \text{med}(D_Y)/\text{med}(D_X)$ (Eq.~3 \cite{li2026scale}) to adjust for the mismatch in modal density. The result --- a dismal $0.5\%$ Top-5, corrected or not --- rules out simple scale discrepancies as the core issue.

\paragraph{Test 6: Rank Constraint Sweep.}
We aggressively swept the rank parameter across seven values, $r \in \{2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200\}$. Performance peaked at rank 50, hitting $2.4\%$ Top-5. In the synthetic Test 1, pushing rank from 20 to 200 improved Case A dramatically, from $19\%$ to $93\%$; nothing comparable happened on real data at any rank. The bottleneck clearly isn't the model's representational capacity --- it's the structural incompatibility of the data itself.

\subsection{Dataset and Architecture Generalisation (Tests 7 to 9)}
\label{subsec:dataset_generalization}

Next we checked whether our specific dataset sampling or architecture choices were somehow to blame for the collapse.

\paragraph{Test 7: Query Set Expansion.}
Maybe a 500-pair query set just wasn't dense enough to help the solver orient the manifolds correctly? We tried $n \in \{500, 1000, 2000\}$. Performance didn't improve with more data --- it actually degraded slightly at $n=2000$ --- so data scarcity isn't the limiting factor either.

\paragraph{Test 8: CCA Dimension Sweep.}
Was the $k=50$ \gls{cca} subspace throwing away too much structural variance? We evaluated $k \in \{10, 20, 50, 100\}$. LR-GW's best result came at $k=20$, at $3.0\%$ Top-5, versus $30.1\%$ for the supervised \gls{cca}+Affine baseline in that same $k=20$ subspace. The gap holds up consistently across every dimensionality we tried.

\paragraph{Test 9: Comprehensive Encoder Matrix.}
To rule out something specific to the PointNet or \gls{clip} topologies, we repeated the standard \gls{lrgw} configuration (rank$=20$, $\epsilon=0.1$, 3 seeds) across all six encoder combinations --- PointNet and SparseConv, each against \gls{clip}, \gls{roberta}, and \gls{bert}. The unsupervised retrieval metrics stayed flat, hovering between $1.1\%$ and $1.7\%$ Top-5 regardless of which encoders were paired. The supervised \gls{cca}+Affine baseline, by contrast, swings widely across the same six pairs, from $10.2\%$ to $34.1\%$ Top-5, tracking real quality differences between encoders --- differences that \gls{lrgw} is completely blind to. This closes the door on any encoder-specific explanation: the \gls{gw} failure is intrinsic to cross-modal alignment itself.

\paragraph{Test 11: Transport Plan Inspection.}
We then looked directly at the raw structure of the transport matrix $T$ itself. A genuinely useful retrieval matrix concentrates most of its probability mass along the diagonal. What we found: the \gls{lrgw} transport plan's diagonal-to-uniform ratio sat between $0.4\times$ and $1.2\times$. For comparison, the pseudo-transport matrix induced by the supervised \gls{cca}+Affine mapping --- built by shifting the cosine-similarity matrix to be non-negative and renormalising it to sum to one, since \gls{cca}+Affine doesn't produce a genuine doubly-stochastic coupling on its own --- shows a diagonal-to-uniform ratio of $1.6\times$, meaningfully above the uniform baseline of $1.0\times$.

Worth being careful here: the raw entropy of this \gls{cca}+Affine pseudo-plan isn't directly comparable to that of a genuine transport plan. Cosine similarities between normalised embeddings vary within a fairly narrow numerical band across the \emph{entire} $n \times n$ matrix, so the resulting pseudo-plan looks globally near-flat in absolute value --- entropy $\approx 12.4$, close to the theoretical maximum $\log(n^2)$ --- even though the \emph{relative ranking} within each row, which is really all that Top-$K$ retrieval cares about, correctly favours the diagonal element. The \gls{lrgw} plan, by contrast, is a genuine Sinkhorn-derived coupling with much lower absolute entropy ($7.4$--$9.6$), reflecting real concentration of probability mass --- just concentrated on the \emph{wrong} cells, consistent with the many-to-one collapse discussed earlier. So the diagonal-to-uniform ratio ends up being the more informative and genuinely comparable statistic between the two methods, since it's normalised against the same $n^2$-cell baseline in both cases regardless of the underlying matrix's overall shape. By that measure, the supervised baseline shows real (if imperfect) structural concentration, while the unsupervised \gls{lrgw} solver simply doesn't.

\subsection{Advanced Methodological Verifications (Tests 12 and 13)}
\label{subsec:advanced_verifications}

After reviewing Tests 1 through 11, two final experiments were designed on the supervisor's specific instructions, to close off any remaining methodological loopholes.

\paragraph{Test 12: GW on Raw Features (Pre-CCA, Zero Supervision).}
One reasonable objection to Tests 1--11 was that the \gls{cca} projection --- trained explicitly on 30,000 anchor pairs --- might be linearly filtering out complex structural geometries that the non-linear $\mathcal{GW}$ could otherwise have exploited. If that were true, \gls{gw} on the raw features might succeed exactly where post-\gls{cca} \gls{gw} fails. \paragraph{Test 13: Fine Epsilon Sweep in the Low Range.}
Finally, the supervisor asked for a fine-grained sweep in the low epsilon regime, where \gls{lrgw} gets close to exact, un-regularised optimal transport --- the idea being that if excessive entropic smoothing were responsible for the failure, very small epsilon values ought to reveal it. This is about as clear a computational signature of a flat loss landscape as you're going to get: the solver isn't stuck because of excessive entropy --- it's converging to the same structural mapping because no better semantic alignment exists geometrically, even in the near-unregularised regime.

\section{Theoretical Analysis: Why GW Cannot Work}
\label{sec:why_gw_fails}

The ablation study in Section~\ref{sec:ablation_study} systematically eliminated every conventional point of computational failure. With implementation, hyperparameters, and libraries all validated, the persistent inability of Gromov-Wasserstein to align 3D objects with textual descriptions has to trace back to a fundamental violation of the mathematical assumptions underlying the Optimal Transport framework itself.

This section works out the theoretical reasons behind that collapse, setting the intrinsic topological mismatch of the latent spaces against the success of the supervised linear baseline.

\subsection{The Absence of Structural Isometry}
\label{subsec:isometry_bottleneck}

The whole theoretical case for using the Gromov-Wasserstein distance rests on its ability to bypass heterogeneous coordinate systems by matching relational intra-modal distance matrices instead. But a foundational theorem from Vayer et al.~\cite{vayer2019optimal} proves that the \gls{gw} distance between two spaces converges to a meaningful zero-cost minimum \textit{if and only if} those spaces are globally isometric --- meaning there's a rigorous, distance-preserving bijection connecting their structures.

Our empirical results make it clear that the PointNet and \gls{clip} latent spaces don't share an isometric topology. Hadgi et al.~\cite{hadgi2025escaping} back this up theoretically: they evaluated structural similarity between these unaligned spaces using Linear Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA), and while standard functional vision-text models typically land somewhere between $0.30$ and $0.48$ on that scale, the unaligned PointNet and \gls{clip} spaces scored just $0.12$ (their Figure 2) --- prompting the original authors to conclude outright that uni-modal 3D encoders don't converge to structures resembling text. That's a very low correlation, and it quantifies a genuinely deep structural disconnect.

Applying Li et al.'s methodology~\cite{li2026scale} --- who use \gls{gw} as a training-free thermometer for latent space compatibility rather than as an alignment tool in its own right --- our encoders consistently converged to a \gls{gw} cost of around $0.031$ to $0.032$ post-CCA, at rank $r=200$ (matching the self-GW validation protocol of Test 2, Section~\ref{subsec:implementation_validation}). Don't confuse this with the \gls{gw} cost of $0.0392$ reported in Tests 3 and 13 (Sections~\ref{subsec:hyperparameter_sweeps} and \ref{subsec:advanced_verifications}) --- that number was computed at the much lower rank $r=20$ used for the entropic regularisation sweeps, and lower-rank solvers systematically settle on higher residual costs because of the coarser transport plan approximation they're forced into. Table~\ref{tab:gw-diagnostic} lays out this diagnostic breakdown across all six encoder pairs, and it's striking: raw, pre-normalisation metric scales are wildly incompatible --- scale factors range from $s \approx 0.09$ all the way to $s \approx 228{,}000$ --- yet every pair converges to essentially the same near-uninformative \gls{gw} cost ($\approx 0.031$--$0.032$) once projected into the shared \gls{cca} subspace.

\subsection{Final Method Ranking and The Necessity of Supervision}
\label{subsec:necessity_supervision}

The fundamental split between the successful linear baseline and the failed optimal transport solvers comes down entirely to how each one exploits ground-truth correspondence. The affine map spends 100\% of its objective on anchor labels: it minimises $\|Rx + b - y\|^2$ directly over known pairs, with no structural term at all, and it doesn't need the two spaces to be geometrically similar to begin with. \gls{gw}, by contrast, has no access to labels whatsoever and can only exploit structural similarity --- which our thirteen tests and \gls{cka} analysis both show to be simply absent here.

Why does the \gls{cca} + Affine pipeline succeed where \gls{gw} fails? The affine mapping $T(x) = Rx + b$ strictly minimises the mathematical discrepancy between predefined semantic anchor pairs, completely overriding the natural global topology of the manifolds --- it doesn't need the 3D space to resemble the text space geometrically at all, because it just forces the correlation through supervised constraints instead.

Gromov-Wasserstein, meanwhile, operates blind, with no labels to lean on. All it can exploit is structural similarity, and as the \gls{cka} scores and our thirteen ablation tests both make clear, that similarity is fundamentally absent between raw 3D coordinate representations and linguistic transformer embeddings.

Trying to bridge the semantic gap between non-isometric modalities using purely unsupervised structural alignment turns out to be a genuine theoretical dead end. Explicit semantic supervision isn't optional here --- it's an absolute mathematical necessity for functional 3D-to-text retrieval in these specific latent spaces. The non-linear supervised methods of Chapter~\ref{ch:nonlinear} reinforce this further: even the most expressive non-linear alternatives (\gls{rml} at 27.2\%, \gls{kcca} at 15.8\%) still fall short of the simple linear affine map (35.7\%), which tells us the performance ceiling here is set by the quality of the supervision, not by how complex the alignment model is.
