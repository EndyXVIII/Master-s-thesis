\section{Dataset: Objaverse}
\label{sec:dataset_objaverse}

Gauging the structural alignment between 3D object shapes and text descriptions calls for a massive dataset of multimodal paired data. For this study, we selected Objaverse \cite{deitke2023objaverse}, the most extensive public repository of multimodal 3D models paired with natural language captions, generated through the Cap3D captioning framework \cite{luo2023scalable}. Earlier synthetic datasets tended to be restricted to a handful of fixed, rigid object classes; Objaverse instead offers a genuinely comprehensive and realistic geometric and semantic distribution, providing the necessary foundation to properly study the correlation between these cross-modal latent spaces.

\subsection{Feature Extraction Pipeline}
\label{subsec:feature_pipeline}

The entire experimental workflow ran on the University of Padova's high-performance computing (HPC) cluster, \texttt{labsrv7}. Feature extraction and pair alignment were distributed across NVIDIA A100, Tesla V100, and Tesla T4 GPUs, managed through the Slurm workload management system.

We started by sampling $100{,}000$ 3D objects from Objaverse. Each mesh was standardised prior to feature extraction: we uniformly sampled $2{,}048$ surface points, centred the sample by subtracting the point cloud's mean, and then normalised it by the maximum value along its Euclidean dimensions. The pipeline processed objects in batches of $10{,}000$, incorporating an auto-resume mechanism to handle node failures or job interruptions inherent to shared HPC environments. After filtering out meshes with errors and samples whose associated text was missing or nonsensical, we established a final dataset of $46{,}675$ paired samples carrying a clean textual annotation across every modality.

\paragraph{Preprocessing Details.} Beyond the point-cloud standardisation described above, each modality underwent an additional normalisation step before fitting the alignment models. All 3D and text feature vectors were $L_2$-normalised to unit norm immediately after extraction, ensuring that the cosine-similarity-based retrieval metrics used later (Section~\ref{subsec:topk_acc}) would not be disproportionately influenced by differences in the raw magnitude of embeddings generated by architecturally diverse encoders. For methods requiring zero-mean input --- specifically the raw-feature ablation of Test 12 in Chapter~\ref{ch:gw_alignment} --- we also applied a StandardScaler transformation (zero mean, unit variance per dimension, fitted exclusively on the anchor split and applied unchanged to the query split). This isolated whether the normalisation scheme itself was driving any of the observed performance differences. No outlier removal or clipping was applied at any stage; given the diagnostic nature of this thesis, the raw encoder outputs were left as unaltered as possible.

\subsection{Anchor Pairs and Query Protocol}
\label{subsec:anchor_protocol}

Following the approach of \textcite{hadgi2025escaping}, we partitioned the dataset into a strictly defined anchoring subset and querying subset --- $n_A = 30{,}000$ and $500$ matched pairs, respectively --- and used them to extract the alignment spaces and evaluate the resulting representations. The anchoring subset drove the explicit fitting of the Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) projection and the construction of the affine mapping from the canonical directions. Meanwhile, the query subset remained entirely unseen, reserved exclusively for evaluating the quality of the resulting alignments. Every quantitative result reported here represents the mean over three independent data splits derived from these anchor/query partitions.

\section{Encoders}
\label{sec:encoders_setup}

To investigate how different neural architectures shape their resulting latent spaces, we assembled a diverse set of feature extractors. The objective was not to chase state-of-the-art performance for its own sake, but rather to understand how architectural priors --- unimodal versus multimodal pre-training, in particular --- dictate the geometric topology of the embedded data.

\subsection{3D Encoders: PointNet and SparseConv}
\label{subsec:3d_encoders}

Processing 3D geometries introduces unique challenges, primarily because raw point clouds lack a regular grid structure and are strictly permutation invariant. To process our standardised $2{,}048$-point samples, we deployed two architecturally distinct geometric backbones.

PointNet served as the primary pre-trained extractor. It processes the raw $(x, y, z)$ coordinates directly, passing them through shared Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs), and aggregates global shape features via symmetric max-pooling to produce a robust $1024$-dimensional feature vector. Its straightforward, global approach makes it a solid baseline for latent space analysis, precisely because the resulting geometry is not heavily distorted by hierarchical inductive biases typically found in more elaborate architectures.

For contrast, we also evaluated a Sparse Convolutional Network (SparseConv). Unlike PointNet's global pooling, SparseConv discretises the 3D space into a sparse voxel grid and restricts convolutions to occupied regions, which theoretically captures fine-grained local geometry more effectively. We configured this backbone to output $512$-dimensional embeddings. Notably --- and this becomes relevant in the baseline evaluations later --- we deliberately deployed SparseConv without any pre-training (using random weight initialisation).\footnote{Pre-trained SparseConv weights were requested from the original authors of \textcite{hadgi2025escaping} but were not available at the time of writing. The random-weight configuration therefore represents a lower bound on SparseConv performance, and results are expected to improve substantially once pre-trained weights become available.} This choice isolates the raw alignment capabilities of the spatial architecture itself, completely separated from anything the network might have otherwise learned.

\subsection{Text Encoders: OpenCLIP, RoBERTa, and BERT}
\label{subsec:text_encoders_setup}

On the textual side, the central question is whether purely linguistic representations can be mapped onto 3D geometry via optimal transport, or whether explicit visual pre-training is a strict requirement for cross-modal alignment. To cover that spectrum, we extracted features from three distinct transformer-based models.

The most robust text backbone in this study comes from OpenCLIP, specifically the \texttt{ViT-bigG-14} variant. Because \gls{clip} is trained contrastively on massive image-text pairs, its latent space inherently encodes physical and visual semantics. We hypothesise that this underlying ``visual grounding'' renders its text embeddings inherently more compatible with 3D structural spaces.

To probe purely linguistic spaces, we also extracted embeddings from \gls{bert} and \gls{roberta}. Both were trained strictly on masked language modelling objectives over massive text corpora, with zero exposure to visual, spatial, or geometric data. \gls{bert} acts as our standard uni-modal baseline, while \gls{roberta} represents a heavily optimised evolution of the same underlying architecture. Comparing how these pure language models align against \gls{clip} allows us to gauge the extent to which cross-modal translation success depends on the text encoder's prior geometric exposure. This hypothesis is directly supported by the results in Chapter~\ref{ch:baseline}, where \gls{clip}-paired encoders consistently and substantially outperform the purely linguistic alternatives across every evaluation metric.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

Quantifying cross-modal alignment efficacy requires capturing both strict one-to-one correspondences and broader semantic retrieval capability. To keep the evaluation directly comparable with the supervised linear baseline, we assess the aligned representations using two primary criteria, applied to our held-out query set of $500$ pairs.

\subsection{Matching Accuracy}
\label{subsec:matching_acc}

Matching accuracy is the strictest metric employed. We frame the evaluation as a bipartite matching problem: given a completely shuffled set of 500 query point clouds and their 500 corresponding text embeddings, the algorithm must reconstruct the exact original pairs.

To achieve this, we first compute a dense pairwise distance matrix between all projected 3D and text features. We then apply the Hungarian algorithm (Linear Sum Assignment) to find the global permutation that minimises the total cost across the batch. Matching accuracy is simply the percentage of correctly recovered pairs. While mathematically elegant, it is a genuinely harsh metric. In a highly diverse dataset like Objaverse, two distinct objects can share nearly identical geometries or semantic descriptions. Consequently, the algorithm is heavily penalised for interchanging one generic ``wooden chair'' with another equally generic ``wooden chair'' located elsewhere in the batch.

\subsection{Top-K Retrieval Accuracy}
\label{subsec:topk_acc}

Since exact matching can be needlessly rigid for a task of this nature, Top-$K$ retrieval serves as the primary indicator of practical alignment quality. Rather than forcing a global one-to-one assignment, this metric evaluates the local neighbourhood surrounding each embedding.

For a given 3D query shape, we rank all 500 text embeddings by cosine distance (or Euclidean distance, depending on the space's topology) from the query point. If the ground-truth caption lands within the top $K$ nearest neighbours, the retrieval is counted as successful. We report results for $K \in \{1, 5, 10\}$.

This metric proves particularly useful for interpreting latent spaces. A high Top-5 or Top-10 score alongside a lower Top-1 score suggests the underlying geometry is successfully clustering similar concepts together, even if the absolute nearest neighbour is subject to local noise. As we transition into the Optimal Transport framework in the subsequent chapters, these retrieval metrics clearly indicate whether Gromov-Wasserstein distances preserve these semantic neighbourhoods or collapse the space entirely.

\section{Diagnostic Metrics}
\label{sec:diagnostic_metrics}

Matching Accuracy and Top-$K$ Retrieval (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}) indicate \emph{how well} an alignment method performs, but they remain silent on \emph{why} a method succeeds or fails. To support the diagnostic analysis running through Chapter~\ref{ch:gw_alignment} --- the thirteen-test ablation study in particular, and the structural non-isometry argument of Section~\ref{sec:why_gw_fails} --- this section formally introduces two complementary diagnostic tools: Centered Kernel Alignment, which directly quantifies structural similarity between two representation spaces, and transport plan entropy, which characterises the internal structure of a learned coupling matrix independently of the resulting retrieval accuracy.

\subsection{Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA)}
\label{subsec:cka_definition}

Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA), introduced by \textcite{kornblith2019similarity}, measures the similarity between two sets of representations of the same $n$ samples, even when those representations have completely different dimensionalities. Given two feature matrices $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_1}$ and $Y \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_2}$, and two kernel matrices $K = k(X,X)$ and $L = l(Y,Y)$ computed over the two spaces, CKA is defined via the Hilbert-Schmidt Independence Criterion (HSIC) \cite{gretton2005measuring}:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{CKA}(K, L) = \frac{\mathrm{HSIC}(K, L)}{\sqrt{\mathrm{HSIC}(K, K) \, \mathrm{HSIC}(L, L)}}
    \label{eq:cka}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{HSIC}(K, L) = \frac{1}{(n-1)^2} \mathrm{tr}(K H L H)$, and $H = I_n - \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top$ is the centering matrix. In the special, computationally convenient case of a linear kernel ($K = XX^\top$, $L = YY^\top$), this reduces to a much simpler closed-form expression:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{CKA}_{\mathrm{linear}}(X, Y) = \frac{\| Y^\top X \|_F^2}{\| X^\top X \|_F \, \| Y^\top Y \|_F}
    \label{eq:linear_cka}
\end{equation}
which is the variant we use throughout this thesis. CKA is bounded in $[0, 1]$: a score of $0$ means no structural correlation between the two spaces at all, while a score of $1$ means the pairwise geometric relationships of one space are perfectly recoverable from the other, up to an orthogonal transformation and isotropic scaling. Critically, unlike the Gromov-Wasserstein distance of Section~\ref{sec:gw_distance}, computing CKA requires the two representation sets to be sampled over the \emph{same} underlying set of $n$ objects --- it requires known correspondences to be computed at all. This precludes its use as an unsupervised \emph{alignment} tool, but makes it ideally suited as a supervised \emph{diagnostic}: computed directly on the known 3D-text anchor pairs, it provides a ground-truth measurement of how structurally similar the two \emph{unaligned} latent spaces already are, independently of whatever alignment method is subsequently applied to them. This is precisely the role CKA fulfils in the analysis of Section~\ref{subsec:isometry_bottleneck}, where the CKA scores reported by \textcite{hadgi2025escaping} between several 3D and text encoder combinations serve as independent corroborating evidence for the absence of structural isometry inferred from our own Gromov-Wasserstein ablation results.

\subsection{Transport Plan Entropy and Diagonal Concentration}
\label{subsec:entropy_definition}

A second diagnostic tool, used extensively in the ablation study of Chapter~\ref{ch:gw_alignment} (Test 11, Section~\ref{subsec:library_analysis}), looks directly at the internal structure of a learned transport plan $T \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, independently of the resulting retrieval accuracy. Two complementary statistics do the job here.

\paragraph{Transport Plan Entropy.} As introduced back in the entropic regularisation of Section~\ref{subsec:sinkhorn}, the (discrete) entropy of a transport plan is:
\begin{equation}
    H(T) = -\sum_{i,j} T_{ij} \log T_{ij}
    \label{eq:plan_entropy}
\end{equation}
A transport plan that concentrates all its mass on a single, semantically correct permutation of matches --- the ideal outcome for cross-modal retrieval --- has minimal entropy; in the extreme case of a perfect permutation matrix, $H(T) = 0$. Conversely, a maximally uninformative plan --- one that spreads mass uniformly across every possible pairing ($T_{ij} = 1/n^2$ for all $i,j$) --- reaches the theoretical maximum entropy $H_{\max} = \log(n^2)$. A learned plan whose entropy sits close to that maximum is, statistically speaking, indistinguishable from random guessing, regardless of the computed retrieval accuracy. This is precisely the diagnostic signature used to characterise the failure of unsupervised Gromov-Wasserstein solvers in Test 11.

\paragraph{Diagonal-to-Uniform Concentration Ratio.} Since the ground-truth correspondence between a query set of $n$ 3D objects and their $n$ paired captions is, by construction, the identity permutation (query $i$ matches caption $i$), a second, highly interpretable diagnostic evaluates how much probability mass a transport plan places on its own diagonal relative to the uniform baseline:
\begin{equation}
    \rho(T) = \frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i} T_{ii}}{\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i,j} T_{ij}}
    \label{eq:diagonal_ratio}
\end{equation}
This measures the mean diagonal mass of $T$ relative to what a uniform, entirely uninformative coupling would produce. A ratio of $\rho(T) = 1.0$ means the transport plan is indistinguishable from uniform noise, while $\rho(T) > 1$ signals a genuine --- if imperfect --- tendency to recover the correct correspondences. We report this ratio alongside CCA+Affine's implicit transport structure and the unsupervised GW solvers' output plans in Section~\ref{subsec:library_analysis}, giving a direct, retrieval-metric-independent confirmation that the unsupervised solvers are not recovering any meaningful structural signal.
