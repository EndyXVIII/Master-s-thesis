\section{Dataset: Objaverse}
\label{sec:dataset_objaverse}

Gauging the structural alignment between 3D object shapes and text descriptions calls for a massive dataset of multimodal paired data. For this, we settled on Objaverse \cite{deitke2023objaverse}, the most extensive public repository of multimodal 3D models paired with natural language captions, generated through the Cap3D captioning framework \cite{luo2023scalable}. Earlier synthetic datasets tended to be restricted to a handful of fixed, rigid object classes; Objaverse instead offers a genuinely comprehensive and realistic geometric and semantic distribution, which is exactly what's needed to study the correlation between these cross-modal latent spaces properly.

\subsection{Feature Extraction Pipeline}
\label{subsec:feature_pipeline}

The whole experimental workflow ran on the University of Padova's high-performance computing (HPC) cluster, \texttt{labsrv7}. Feature extraction and pair alignment were spread across NVIDIA A100, Tesla V100, and Tesla T4 GPUs, all managed through the Slurm workload management system.

We start by sampling $100{,}000$ 3D objects from Objaverse. Each mesh gets standardised before any feature extraction happens: we uniformly sample $2{,}048$ surface points, centre the sample by subtracting the point cloud's mean, then normalise by the maximum value along its Euclidean dimensions. The pipeline processes objects in batches of $10{,}000$, with an auto-resume mechanism built in for whenever a node fails or a job gets interrupted --- which, on a shared HPC cluster, happens more often than you'd like. After filtering out meshes with errors and samples whose associated text was missing or nonsensical, we ended up with $46{,}675$ paired samples carrying a clean textual annotation across every modality.

\paragraph{Preprocessing Details.} Beyond the point-cloud standardisation above, each modality went through one more normalisation step before any alignment method was fitted. All 3D and text feature vectors were $L_2$-normalised to unit norm right after extraction, so that the cosine-similarity-based retrieval metrics used later (Section~\ref{subsec:topk_acc}) wouldn't be thrown off by differences in the raw magnitude of embeddings coming from architecturally very different encoders. For methods that need zero-mean input --- specifically the raw-feature ablation of Test 12 in Chapter~\ref{ch:gw_alignment} --- we also tried a StandardScaler transformation (zero mean, unit variance per dimension, fitted on the anchor split only and applied unchanged to the query split), just to isolate whether the normalisation scheme itself was driving any of the observed performance differences. No outlier removal or clipping was applied anywhere: since the whole point of this thesis is diagnostic, we wanted the raw encoder outputs left as untouched as possible.

\subsection{Anchor Pairs and Query Protocol}
\label{subsec:anchor_protocol}

Following \textcite{hadgi2025escaping}'s approach, we split the dataset into a strictly-defined anchoring subset and querying subset --- $n_A = 30{,}000$ and $500$ matched pairs, respectively --- and use them to extract the alignment spaces and evaluate the resulting representations. The anchoring subset drives the explicit fitting of the Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) projection and the affine-mapping construction from the canonical directions, while the query subset stays entirely unseen, reserved purely for evaluating how good the resulting alignments actually are. Every quantitative result reported here is the mean over three independent data splits built from these anchor/query partitions.

\section{Encoders}
\label{sec:encoders_setup}

To really dig into how different neural architectures shape their resulting latent spaces, we assembled a fairly diverse set of feature extractors. The goal wasn't to chase state-of-the-art performance for its own sake, but to understand how architectural priors --- unimodal versus multimodal pre-training, in particular --- end up dictating the geometric topology of the embedded data.

\subsection{3D Encoders: PointNet and SparseConv}
\label{subsec:3d_encoders}

Processing 3D geometries comes with its own headaches, mostly because raw point clouds lack any regular grid structure and are strictly permutation invariant. To handle our standardised $2{,}048$-point samples, we deployed two architecturally quite different geometric backbones.

PointNet served as our primary pre-trained extractor. It consumes the raw $(x, y, z)$ coordinates directly, passes them through shared Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs), and aggregates global shape features via symmetric max-pooling, producing a robust $1024$-dimensional feature vector. Its straightforward, global approach makes it a solid baseline for latent space analysis, precisely because the resulting geometry isn't heavily distorted by hierarchical inductive biases the way a more elaborate architecture's might be.

To contrast this, we also brought in a Sparse Convolutional Network (SparseConv). Unlike PointNet's global pooling, SparseConv discretises the 3D space into a sparse voxel grid and restricts convolutions to occupied regions, which in principle should capture fine-grained local geometry much better. We configured this backbone to output $512$-dimensional embeddings. Notably --- and this becomes relevant in the baseline evaluations later --- we deliberately used SparseConv without any pre-training at all (random weight initialisation).\footnote{Pre-trained SparseConv weights were requested from the original authors of \textcite{hadgi2025escaping} but were not available at the time of writing. The random-weight configuration therefore represents a lower bound on SparseConv performance, and results are expected to improve substantially once pre-trained weights become available.} This choice lets us isolate the raw alignment capabilities of the spatial architecture itself, completely separated from anything the network might have learned.

\subsection{Text Encoders: OpenCLIP, RoBERTa, and BERT}
\label{subsec:text_encoders_setup}

On the textual side, the central question is whether purely linguistic representations can be mapped onto 3D geometry via optimal transport, or whether explicit visual pre-training is really a hard requirement for cross-modal alignment. To cover that spectrum, we extracted features from three distinct transformer-based models.

Our strongest text backbone comes from OpenCLIP, specifically the \texttt{ViT-bigG-14} variant. Because \gls{clip} is trained contrastively on massive image-text pairs, its latent space inherently encodes physical and visual semantics --- we suspect this underlying ``visual grounding'' is exactly what makes its text embeddings more compatible with 3D structural spaces.

To probe purely linguistic spaces, we also extracted embeddings from \gls{bert} and \gls{roberta}. Both were trained strictly on masked language modelling objectives over massive text corpora, with zero exposure to visual, spatial, or geometric data of any kind. \gls{bert} acts as our standard uni-modal baseline, while \gls{roberta} represents a heavily optimised evolution of the same underlying architecture. Comparing how these pure language models align against \gls{clip} lets us gauge how much of the cross-modal translation success actually comes down to the text encoder already ``knowing'' what the physical world looks like --- a hypothesis borne out directly by the results in Chapter~\ref{ch:baseline}, where \gls{clip}-paired encoders consistently and substantially outperform the purely linguistic alternatives across every evaluation metric.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

Quantifying how well cross-modal alignment actually works means capturing both strict one-to-one correspondences and broader semantic retrieval capability. To keep the evaluation strictly comparable with the supervised linear baseline, we assess the aligned representations using two primary criteria, applied to our held-out query set of $500$ pairs.

\subsection{Matching Accuracy}
\label{subsec:matching_acc}

Matching accuracy is the strictest metric we use. We frame the evaluation as a bipartite matching problem: given a completely shuffled set of 500 query point clouds and their 500 corresponding text embeddings, the algorithm has to reconstruct the exact original pairs.

To do this, we first compute a dense pairwise distance matrix between all projected 3D and text features, then apply the Hungarian algorithm (Linear Sum Assignment) to find the global permutation minimising total cost across the batch. Matching accuracy is simply the percentage of correctly recovered pairs. It's mathematically elegant, but genuinely harsh as a metric --- in a dataset as diverse as Objaverse, two distinct objects can share very similar geometry or near-identical semantic descriptions, so the algorithm gets penalised heavily for confusing one generic ``wooden chair'' with another equally generic ``wooden chair'' sitting elsewhere in the batch.

\subsection{Top-K Retrieval Accuracy}
\label{subsec:topk_acc}

Because exact matching can be needlessly rigid for a multimodal task like this, Top-$K$ retrieval ends up being our primary indicator of practical alignment quality. Rather than forcing a global one-to-one assignment, it tests the local neighbourhood around each embedding instead.

For a given 3D query shape, we rank all 500 text embeddings by cosine distance (or Euclidean distance, depending on the space's topology) from the query point. If the ground-truth caption lands within the top $K$ nearest neighbours, we count the retrieval as successful, and we report results for $K \in \{1, 5, 10\}$.

This metric turns out to be particularly useful for reading latent spaces. A high Top-5 or Top-10 score alongside a lower Top-1 suggests the underlying geometry is still successfully clustering similar concepts together, even if the single nearest neighbour is a bit noisy. As we move into the Optimal Transport framework in the chapters ahead, these retrieval metrics will make it very clear whether Gromov-Wasserstein distances actually preserve these semantic neighbourhoods, or whether they collapse the space entirely.

\section{Diagnostic Metrics}
\label{sec:diagnostic_metrics}

Matching Accuracy and Top-$K$ Retrieval (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}) tell us \emph{how well} an alignment method performs, but they're silent on \emph{why} a method succeeds or fails. To support the theoretical and diagnostic analysis running through Chapter~\ref{ch:gw_alignment} --- the thirteen-test ablation study in particular, and the structural non-isometry argument of Section~\ref{sec:why_gw_fails} --- this section formally introduces two complementary diagnostic tools: Centered Kernel Alignment, which directly quantifies structural similarity between two representation spaces, and transport plan entropy, which characterises the internal structure of a learned coupling matrix independently of whatever retrieval accuracy it happens to produce.

\subsection{Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA)}
\label{subsec:cka_definition}

Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA), introduced by \textcite{kornblith2019similarity}, measures the similarity between two sets of representations of the same $n$ samples, even when those representations have completely different dimensionalities. Given two feature matrices $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_1}$ and $Y \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_2}$, and two kernel matrices $K = k(X,X)$ and $L = l(Y,Y)$ computed over the two spaces, CKA is defined via the Hilbert-Schmidt Independence Criterion (HSIC) \cite{gretton2005measuring}:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{CKA}(K, L) = \frac{\mathrm{HSIC}(K, L)}{\sqrt{\mathrm{HSIC}(K, K) \, \mathrm{HSIC}(L, L)}}
    \label{eq:cka}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{HSIC}(K, L) = \frac{1}{(n-1)^2} \mathrm{tr}(K H L H)$, and $H = I_n - \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top$ is the centering matrix. In the special, computationally convenient case of a linear kernel ($K = XX^\top$, $L = YY^\top$), this reduces to a much simpler closed-form expression:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{CKA}_{\mathrm{linear}}(X, Y) = \frac{\| Y^\top X \|_F^2}{\| X^\top X \|_F \, \| Y^\top Y \|_F}
    \label{eq:linear_cka}
\end{equation}
which is the variant we use throughout this thesis. CKA is bounded in $[0, 1]$: a score of $0$ means no structural correlation between the two spaces at all, while a score of $1$ means the pairwise geometric relationships of one space are perfectly recoverable from the other, up to an orthogonal transformation and isotropic scaling. What's crucial here is that, unlike the Gromov-Wasserstein distance of Section~\ref{sec:gw_distance}, computing CKA requires the two representation sets to be sampled over the \emph{same} underlying set of $n$ objects --- it needs known correspondences to work at all. That rules it out as an unsupervised \emph{alignment} tool, but makes it ideally suited as a supervised \emph{diagnostic}: computed directly on the known 3D-text anchor pairs, it gives a ground-truth measurement of how structurally similar the two \emph{unaligned} latent spaces already are, independently of whatever alignment method gets applied to them afterward. That's exactly the role CKA plays in the theoretical analysis of Section~\ref{subsec:isometry_bottleneck}, where the low CKA score \textcite{hadgi2025escaping} report between raw PointNet and CLIP embeddings ($\approx 0.12$) serves as independent corroborating evidence for the absence of structural isometry we infer from our own Gromov-Wasserstein ablation results.

\subsection{Transport Plan Entropy and Diagonal Concentration}
\label{subsec:entropy_definition}

A second diagnostic tool, used extensively in the ablation study of Chapter~\ref{ch:gw_alignment} (Test 11, Section~\ref{subsec:library_analysis}), looks directly at the internal structure of a learned transport plan $T \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, independently of the retrieval accuracy it ends up producing. Two complementary statistics do the job here.

\paragraph{Transport Plan Entropy.} As introduced back in the entropic regularisation of Section~\ref{subsec:sinkhorn}, the (discrete) entropy of a transport plan is:
\begin{equation}
    H(T) = -\sum_{i,j} T_{ij} \log T_{ij}
    \label{eq:plan_entropy}
\end{equation}
A transport plan that concentrates all its mass on a single, semantically correct permutation of matches --- the ideal outcome for cross-modal retrieval --- has minimal entropy; in the extreme case of a perfect permutation matrix, $H(T) = 0$. A maximally uninformative plan, on the other hand, one that spreads mass uniformly across every possible pairing ($T_{ij} = 1/n^2$ for all $i,j$), hits the theoretical maximum entropy $H_{\max} = \log(n^2)$. A learned plan whose entropy sits close to that maximum is, statistically speaking, indistinguishable from random guessing, regardless of whatever retrieval accuracy gets computed from it --- and that's precisely the diagnostic signature we use to characterise the failure of unsupervised Gromov-Wasserstein solvers in Test 11.

\paragraph{Diagonal-to-Uniform Concentration Ratio.} Since the ground-truth correspondence between a query set of $n$ 3D objects and their $n$ paired captions is, by construction, the identity permutation (query $i$ matches caption $i$), a second and more interpretable diagnostic looks at how much probability mass a transport plan puts on its own diagonal relative to the uniform baseline:
\begin{equation}
    \rho(T) = \frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i} T_{ii}}{\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i,j} T_{ij}}
    \label{eq:diagonal_ratio}
\end{equation}
This measures the mean diagonal mass of $T$ relative to what a uniform, entirely uninformative coupling would produce. A ratio of $\rho(T) = 1.0$ means the transport plan is indistinguishable from uniform noise, while $\rho(T) > 1$ signals a genuine --- if imperfect --- tendency to recover the correct correspondences. We report this ratio alongside CCA+Affine's implicit transport structure and the unsupervised GW solvers' output plans in Section~\ref{subsec:library_analysis}, giving a direct, retrieval-metric-independent confirmation that the unsupervised solvers simply aren't recovering any meaningful structural signal.
